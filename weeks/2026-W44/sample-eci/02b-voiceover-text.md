## DEEP DIVE 2 — Who decides at the Election Commission?

Save as: `sample-eci_VO_D2.wav`

**🎙️ NARRATION — *calm, measured.***

On the fifth of October, the Supreme Court refused to suspend the Chief Election Commissioner, but issued notice on petitions alleging that he decided without his two colleagues.

On the twenty-sixth, the Commission's affidavit said every order for its Special Intensive Revision was unanimous, and the bench of Chief Justice Surya Kant [SOOR-ya Kaant] listed the case for the seventeenth of November.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

Who is right is for the Court to decide, so our question is structural: is the Election Commission a three-member body that decides together, or an office in which one member is far better protected than the other two?

Two terms first.

A multi-member Commission means the Chief and the two Election Commissioners decide together, with the Chief as the first among equals.

And a Special Intensive Revision prepares the electoral roll afresh, house to house, instead of the routine summary revision.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower on the years.***

Now the static anchor.

Article 324 gives the Commission the superintendence, direction and control of elections, and clause two lets the President fix how many Election Commissioners sit with the Chief.

For forty years the Commission had one member, apart from a brief spell in 1989, and it has had three since October 1993.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

When the then Chief Election Commissioner, T. N. Seshan [SHAY-shun], challenged that change, a five-judge bench upheld it in 1995 and called the Chief the first among equals.

The rule survives in today's law: Section 18 of the 2023 Act says the Commission's business should be unanimous as far as possible, and when members differ, the majority view prevails.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower.***

But here is the paradox.

Article 324, clause five, protects the Chief like a Supreme Court judge, while an Election Commissioner can be removed on the Chief's recommendation.

So the three have equal votes but unequal security, and that is why the Commission's reply to the petitioners relied on clause five.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

The second issue is Form 6, the form a new voter fills in to join the roll.

It sits in the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960, which the Central Government makes under Section 28 of the Representation of the People Act, 1950, after consulting the Commission.

So the Commission cannot amend Form 6 on its own, and its affidavit says the S-I-R declaration is a separate document, not a change to the form.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower, deliberate.***

**Prelims Trap.** UPSC may ask how an Election Commissioner is removed, and the trap is that only the Chief gets the Supreme Court judge procedure; an Election Commissioner goes on the Chief's recommendation.

The second trap is the number, which the President fixes, not the Constitution.

The third is the rule-maker: the Central Government makes the registration rules, and each roll is prepared by an Electoral Registration Officer.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

Now try one in the Statement-one, Statement-two format, and decide which option is right.

The answer is option c.

Statement one is correct under Article 324, clause five, but statement two is wrong, because when the members differ, the majority decides, not the Chief.

**Mains Lens.** On independence, the 2023 Act replaced the Chief Justice, whom the Anoop Baranwal [A-noop Ba-RAAN-wal] judgment had put on the selection panel, with a Union Cabinet Minister, and in September a divided bench sent that challenge towards a larger bench.

On federal balance, the rolls are prepared by State government officers under the Commission's control.

And on rights, wrongful deletions touch the adult suffrage of Article 326, which is why the Court will examine revision complaints State by State.

**🎙️ NARRATION · `EXT`**

Take the proposition that a multi-member Commission is a stronger guardian of free elections.

For it, three minds check one, which is exactly what the Court valued in Seshan's case, and Section 18 gives the majority the final word.

Against it, when only one member has security of tenure, equal votes may be weaker in practice than on paper, and a dissent counts for little if it is never recorded.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

So what should change?

The Law Commission's two hundred and fifty-fifth report, in 2015, recommended giving Election Commissioners the Chief's removal protection, which would end the asymmetry at its root.

And recording dissents and decisions under Section 18 would show how the Commission actually decided, because for India the strategic asset is trust in the referee, and that rests on structure rather than personalities.

**💬 TALK TO THE VIEWER — *slower.***

**Write this down.** Article 324, clause five, for removal; Section 18 of the 2023 Act for the majority rule; Seshan, 1995, for first among equals; and Section 28 of the 1950 Act for who makes the forms.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

Here is the question I would set: is the Election Commission of India a collegial body in law but a single-member body in practice; examine.

Introduce with Article 324 and the 1993 change, build the body on the decision rule, security of tenure, rule-making and appointment, and conclude with the Law Commission's 2015 recommendation.

**Pause and answer.** Which instrument sets how the Commission votes, and which sets how its members are removed, and which of the two could Parliament change by an ordinary law?

**🎙️ NARRATION**

Three points to carry away.

The Commission is collegial by statute and by the Seshan judgment, and the majority decides when members differ.

Its members are not equally protected, because clause five shields only the Chief.

And the forms belong to the Central Government's rules.
