## AP–TG DESK — AP–TG Desk: seven Telugu States topics, exam format

**🎙️ NARRATION — *warm, bright.***

Decode U-P-S-C: unlock your potential, decode your future. Namaskaram, and welcome! This is your Andhra Pradesh and Telangana Desk for week 44: A-P and Telangana current affairs for A-P-P-S-C and T-G-P-S-C Group-1, and for U-P-S-C, turned into the questions the examiner can ask you.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

Let us warm up with a question that we will settle only at our very last stop. Consider three lakes: Kolleru, Chilika and Pulicat. How many of them are freshwater lakes? Note your answer, and hold on to it.

Here is our desk for today: seven topics, from a courtroom in Amaravati to a lake full of birds near Eluru. One question runs through all seven, so keep it in mind: who holds the pen? Who decides, the State or the Centre, the court or the commission? For every topic you get the news, the static facts, the trap, and a Group-1 style question you answer before I do. Keep a score of your ticks, and stay till the end for the examiner's three tricks.

Topic 1, from Polity, and it starts in your own village panchayat: the Backward Classes quota in Andhra Pradesh's local bodies.

Here is why it is in the news. On the 1st of October, the Andhra Pradesh High Court set aside the orders giving Backward Classes 34% of seats in rural local bodies. On the ninth, the Supreme Court dismissed the State's appeal, and the State Cabinet has since adopted its dedicated commission's report. So the real question for us is what test a quota must pass.

Think of it like selection trials for a cricket team, with three rounds. The Supreme Court's triple test, set in the Gawali case of 2021, asks for three things: a dedicated commission must first study backwardness, the quota must then be fixed body by body, and total reservation must stay within 50%. Fail even one round, and the quota does not make the team. The test builds on K. Krishna Murthy in 2010, and on Indra Sawhney's 50% ceiling of 1992.

Prelims Trap, and this one catches many good students. Article 243D, clause 6, only enables a Backward Classes quota in panchayats; it does not make one compulsory, and any quota must still pass the triple test.

Now it is your turn. Take a breath, read each line slowly, and tell me: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is only two: statements 2 and 3, because the Article only enables a quota; it does not require one. If you got that, well done, because that is exactly the distinction the examiner is looking for.

Here is your notes card. Copy these lines in your own handwriting, and the whole topic sits on one page.

And since we are already in a courtroom, topic 2 stays there: an acting Chief Justice for the Telangana High Court, from Polity.

Why in news: Justice P. Sam Koshy [Sam KO-shee] is now the acting Chief Justice of the Telangana High Court, after the Chief Justice was elevated to the Supreme Court. It is like a vice-captain leading the side when the captain moves up: the match goes on, and someone has to make the calls.

Now learn the High Court Articles as one set, the way you remember a phone number you dial every day. Article 214 gives each State a High Court; 217 covers how judges are appointed; 223, the acting Chief Justice; 224, additional and acting judges; 224A, retired judges; and 231, one High Court for two or more States.

Prelims Trap, and here is our question again: who holds the pen? An acting Chief Justice is appointed by the President under Article 223, not by the Chief Justice of India.

Your turn again. Three statements, and you pick the right code: which of these statements are correct?

The answer is option b, one and two only, because Article 224A is about retired judges sitting in High Courts. Two topics, and I hope two ticks!

Notes card. Write the Articles in order, from 214 to 231, and say them aloud once; you will be surprised how well they stay.

Now let us step out of the courtroom and into a home in rural Andhra Pradesh, where an elderly woman is waiting for her pension. Topic 3, our Telugu States Lens, from Social Justice: who really pays Andhra Pradesh's pension?

Why in news: under Andhra Pradesh's N-T-R Bharosa [Bha-ROH-saa] scheme, the State pays an elderly pensioner 4,000 rupees a month, and the Centre's share of that is 200. Put simply, for every 20 rupees she receives, 19 come from the State and one comes from the Centre.

So where does the Centre's rupee come from? From the National Social Assistance Programme of the Rural Development Ministry: 200 rupees for the elderly aged 60 to 79 and 500 from 80; 300 for widows and for persons with severe disabilities; a one-time 20,000 rupees under the family benefit scheme; and 10 kilograms of free grain a month under Annapurna.

Prelims Trap. The name sounds like social justice, and that is exactly what the examiner is counting on. The National Social Assistance Programme is run by the Ministry of Rural Development, not by the Social Justice Ministry.

Pause and test yourself: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is all three, because each statement matches the programme's rules. Yes, sometimes it really is all three, so trust what you know rather than a pattern.

Notes card, and there is a Group-1 Mains question on it for you to practise this week. Even one honest paragraph on it is real progress.

From money that flows to water that flows: topic 4 is about the Krishna and Godavari river boards, from Geography and Polity.

Why in news: the Krishna and Godavari river management boards were scheduled to meet on the 26th and the 30th of October, with the Polavaram [Poh-laa-va-ram] backwater on the Godavari board's agenda.

Now, who settles what? The two boards were created by the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act of 2014, with an Apex Council of the Union Jal Shakti Minister and both Chief Ministers above them, and Polavaram is a national project under the same Act. Water disputes, on the other hand, go to tribunals under a 1956 Act made under Article 262: the Krishna tribunals of 1973 and 2010, and the Godavari tribunal of 1980. Here is the cleanest way to hold it: boards manage, tribunals decide.

Prelims Trap. The river boards are not tribunals under Article 262; they are bodies created by the 2014 Reorganisation Act.

Over to you. Remember, boards manage and tribunals decide: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is all three, because the boards and Polavaram's status come from the 2014 Act, and the tribunals from the 1956 Act. That one line, boards manage, tribunals decide, just earned you a tick.

Notes card. Copy it, and draw a small arrow from the Act to each body; a picture in your notes is worth 10 readings.

And talking of rivers, let us travel upstream, to the border of Telangana and Maharashtra. Topic 5: the Tummidihatti [Tum-mi-di-HUT-ti] barrage, from Geography.

Why in news: on the 26th of October, Telangana's Chief Minister met Maharashtra's in Mumbai over the Tummidihatti barrage. Telangana wants a full reservoir level of 152 metres, against the 148 agreed in 2016, and the two States agreed to a joint committee of officials. 4 metres sounds small, but on a river, 4 metres decides how far the water spreads.

Now trace it on the map with me. The barrage is on the Pranahita [Praa-na-HEE-ta], which forms where the Wardha meets the Wainganga, runs along the Telangana–Maharashtra border, and joins the Godavari at Kaleshwaram.

Prelims Trap. The barrage is not on the Godavari's main stem; it is on the Pranahita, a tributary.

Your turn. Picture the map we just traced, and then decide: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is only one: statement 1, because the Pranahita joins the Godavari, and the barrage is on the Pranahita, not on the main stem. If you chose two, that is fine; a mistake made here is one you will skip in the exam hall.

Notes card. Wardha plus Wainganga makes the Pranahita: write it like a little sum, and it will stay with you.

From a river border, we now go down to the coast, where this week the sea made the news. Topic 6: who runs a disaster response, from Disaster Management.

Why in news: as Cyclone Arnab came ashore on the Godavari delta coast, Andhra Pradesh's response ran through its State Disaster Management Authority.

Picture the system as a three-storey building with a rescue team on call. Under the Disaster Management Act of 2005, the national authority is chaired by the Prime Minister, the State authority by the Chief Minister, and the district authority by the Collector; and the National Disaster Response Force is set up under section 44 of the same Act.

Prelims Trap, and once more, who holds the pen? The State authority is chaired by the Chief Minister, not by the Governor.

Your question. Look for the one word that has been swapped: which of these statements are correct?

The answer is option b, one and three only, because the State authority is chaired by the Chief Minister. Spotting the swapped word is a skill, and you are building it with every question.

Notes card. Prime Minister, Chief Minister, Collector: three floors, three chairs. Copy it.

And we finish where the Krishna and Godavari deltas meet, at a lake that welcomes thousands of migratory birds every winter. Topic 7: Kolleru [Kol-LAY-ru] Lake, from Environment.

Why in news: Andhra Pradesh has asked the Supreme Court's Central Empowered Committee to exclude about 20,000 acres from the Kolleru Wildlife Sanctuary, as reported.

Now meet the lakes that UPSC likes to line up side by side. Kolleru is a freshwater lake between the Krishna and Godavari deltas, a Ramsar site since 2002; Chilika and Pulicat are brackish lagoons; Sambhar is saline; and Loktak is freshwater, with floating phumdis.

Prelims Trap, and it settles our warm-up question too. Kolleru is not a brackish lagoon like Chilika; it is a freshwater lake. Chilika and Pulicat are brackish, so the answer to our warm-up is only one: Kolleru.

Last question of the desk. Make it count: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is only two: statements 2 and 3, because Kolleru is freshwater, not brackish. And that is all seven questions done!

Your last notes card for today. Copy it, and your Telugu States page for this week is complete.

Now, the moment I promised. Look back at all seven traps, and you will see that the examiner used just three tricks. First, who holds the pen? The President, rather than the Chief Justice of India; the Chief Minister, rather than the Governor; Rural Development, rather than Social Justice. Second, allows or requires, manages or decides? Article 243D, clause 6, enables a quota without requiring one, and river boards manage while tribunals decide. And third, main stream or tributary, fresh or brackish? The barrage is on the Pranahita, and Kolleru is freshwater. Learn to see these three tricks, and every Group-1 paper starts to look familiar.
