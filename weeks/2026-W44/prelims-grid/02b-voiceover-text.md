## PRELIMS GRID — Prelims Grid: sixteen topics, exam format

Save as: `prelims-grid_VO_grid.wav`

**🎙️ NARRATION — *warm, bright.***

Decode U-P-S-C: unlock your potential, decode your future. Hello, and welcome! This is your Prelims Grid for week forty-four. Keep your notebook and a pen next to you, because today you are going to do more than watch the news; you are going to practise it.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *brisk, warm.***

Here is the plan. Sixteen topics from one week, and think of it as a train journey: we board on the Andhra coast, where a cyclone has just come ashore, and we get off at the Statue of Unity. At every station you get the news, the static facts the examiner marks, the trap, and a practice question. Keep a score as we go, one tick for every question you get right, and we will look at your total together at the end.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

First station, and it is a stormy one. Topic one: Cyclone Arnab, from Geography and Disaster Management.

Why in news: on the twenty-eighth of October, Cyclone Arnab crossed the Andhra Pradesh and Yanam coast in the Godavari delta as a very severe cyclonic storm. So let us ask the two questions UPSC loves: how is a storm like this born, and who gives it its name?

First, how a cyclone forms. Think of it as a recipe with four ingredients. It needs warm sea water of about twenty-seven degrees or more; enough Coriolis force, which is why cyclones do not form right at the Equator; weak wind shear; and a low-pressure area to start from. Then the engine starts: warm, moist air rises and condenses, and the heat it releases lowers the pressure at the centre, so more air spirals in, anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere, and the storm keeps feeding itself.

Now the naming process, and it is simpler than it sounds. Thirteen countries of the W-M-O and ESCAP panel gave thirteen names each, a list of one hundred and sixty-nine adopted in 2020. The I-M-D's centre in New Delhi gives the next name only when a storm reaches cyclonic-storm strength, and a name is never used again. And Arnab? That was Bangladesh's name.

The I-M-D grades storms by wind speed, like the steps of a ladder: a cyclonic storm starts at sixty-two kilometres an hour, and a very severe one, like Arnab, runs from one hundred and eighteen to one hundred and sixty-six.

Prelims Trap, and it hides in plain sight on the map. Yanam sits inside Andhra Pradesh, but it belongs to the Union Territory of Puducherry, with Karaikal and Mahe.

Now, your first question of the day. Read each line slowly and decide: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is only two: statements one and three are correct, because Yanam belongs to Puducherry, not to Andhra Pradesh. Got it? Give yourself your first tick.

Here is your notes card. Copy it in your own handwriting; that small effort is what makes it stay.

Next station: from the coast to the markets. Topic two: the IDBI Bank sale, from the Economy.

Why in news: on the fourteenth of October, the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs approved selling sixty point seven two per cent of IDBI Bank, the Government's and L-I-C's stakes together, to a Fairfax company for about fifty-two thousand eight hundred crore rupees.

This is a strategic disinvestment, so learn the chain, like a relay race with three runners. The Department of Investment and Public Asset Management, under the Finance Ministry, runs the sale; the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs approves it; and because the buyer is taking over a bank, the Reserve Bank must still clear it as fit and proper.

Prelims Trap. IDBI is not a public sector bank today: in 2019, after L-I-C took majority control, the R-B-I reclassified it as a private sector bank. Many of us still carry the old label in our heads, so update yours today.

Your turn. One word decides this one, so read carefully: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is only two: statements one and two are correct, because DIPAM sits in the Finance Ministry, not the Commerce Ministry. Ministries are a favourite swap, so that tick is well earned.

Notes card. A few lines, and the whole sale is in your notebook.

And while we are with money, let us follow the dollar. Topic three: the R-B-I's new forex reserve, from the Economy.

Why in news: on the tenth of October, the R-B-I asked banks to keep a twenty per cent reserve, in cash with the R-B-I and earning no interest, on specified rupee forex derivative contracts above two million dollars. It also cut, from one hundred million to five million dollars, the limit up to which users can deal without proving an underlying exposure.

These rules come under FEMA, so let us meet the older law it replaced. FERA, of 1973, treated forex violations as criminal offences, to conserve scarce foreign exchange. FEMA, of 1999, in force from June 2000, made them civil, with penalties and compounding, and both are enforced by the Enforcement Directorate. In one line: FERA punished, FEMA manages.

Prelims Trap. So the new reserve is a FEMA measure, not a FERA one: FERA was repealed, and forex violations are no longer criminal offences.

Pause and try this one, and trust what you just learnt: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is all three, because FEMA is a civil law, the Enforcement Directorate enforces it, and the new reserve earns no interest. Yes, all three can be right; when you know the facts, choose that option with confidence.

Notes card. FERA criminal, FEMA civil: write that one in big letters.

From the economy to the air we breathe, because this week Delhi's air slipped into the poor band again. Topic four: Delhi's air and the Graded Response Action Plan, from Environment.

Why in news: on the twenty-first of October, Delhi's air quality index reached two hundred and forty-four, in the poor band, and Stage One of the Graded Response Action Plan was invoked across the National Capital Region.

The index runs in bands from good up to severe, and the plan climbs like a staircase in four steps: Stage One at poor, from two hundred and one; Stage Two at very poor, from three hundred and one; Stage Three at severe, from four hundred and one; and Stage Four above four hundred and fifty.

Prelims Trap. The plan is invoked by the Commission for Air Quality Management, a statutory body under a 2021 Act, not by the Central Pollution Control Board, which only publishes the index.

Quick check before we move on: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is only one: statement one, because the Commission invokes the plan, and the index tracks eight pollutants, not two. If you caught that eight, you are already reading like an examiner.

Notes card. Copy the four stages as a little staircase in your notebook.

Now a topic that is really about character, the quiet foundation of every good administrator. Topic five: Vigilance Awareness Week, from Polity and Governance.

Why in news: the Central Vigilance Commission is marking Vigilance Awareness Week from the twenty-sixth of October to the first of November, the week of Sardar Patel's birth anniversary, with the theme Probity for Prosperity.

Now compare the four anti-corruption bodies UPSC likes to mix up, like four siblings in matching clothes. The Commission was set up in 1964 on the Santhanam Committee's advice and became statutory under a 2003 Act; the Lokpal comes from a 2013 Act; the C-B-I is an executive body under a 1946 Act; and the C-A-G is the only constitutional one, under Article one hundred and forty-eight.

Prelims Trap. The C-V-C is not a constitutional body; it is statutory, under the Central Vigilance Commission Act of 2003.

Here is your question. Take your time: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is all three, because the Commission began in 1964, became statutory in 2003, and its members are appointed by the President on a committee's recommendation. Four siblings, and now you can tell them apart.

Notes card: four bodies, four origins, one page.

From India's offices to India's oceans. Topic six: India and SIOFA, from International Relations and Environment.

Why in news: in mid-October, India approved joining the Southern Indian Ocean Fisheries Agreement as a full party, after being a cooperating non-party since 2022.

Three bodies share this ocean, and the examiner loves to swap them. SIOFA covers non-tuna fish on the high seas, with its secretariat on Réunion; the Indian Ocean Tuna Commission, an F-A-O body based in the Seychelles, manages tuna; and CCAMLR, based in Hobart, protects Antarctic marine life.

Prelims Trap. SIOFA does not manage tuna; tuna in the Indian Ocean is the job of the Indian Ocean Tuna Commission, of which India is already a member.

Your turn: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is only two: statements two and three, because tuna belongs to the Tuna Commission, not to SIOFA. A simple hook for the exam hall: if it is tuna, think Tuna Commission.

Notes card. Three bodies, three jobs: copy them side by side.

From the fish in the sea to the food on our plates. Topic seven: the Global Hunger Index, from Social Issues.

Why in news: the Global Hunger Index for 2026 ranks India one hundredth of one hundred and twenty-two countries, with a score of twenty-four point one, in the serious category, and the Government disputes its method.

The index adds four indicators: undernourishment and child mortality carry a third each, and child stunting and child wasting a sixth each. A score from twenty to thirty-five counts as serious.

Prelims Trap. The index is not published by the F-A-O or the World Food Programme; it comes from aid organisations led by Welthungerhilfe, while the F-A-O and its partners publish a different report, the SOFI.

Pause and try this one: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is only one: statement two, because Welthungerhilfe-led groups publish the index, and India's score is in the serious band. Who publishes what is a pattern UPSC repeats, and you will meet it again a few stations from now.

Notes card. Four indicators, one publisher: copy it.

Next station, a friendship in the news. Topic eight: Poland and the C-D-R-I, from International Relations.

Why in news: Poland's Prime Minister visited India from the twenty-first to the twenty-third of October; the two sides set a goal to double trade by 2030, and Poland announced it will join the C-D-R-I.

Now the India-led alliances, a favourite look-alike set and a story of India's growing voice. The International Solar Alliance was launched in 2015 at the Paris climate summit with France, and sits in Gurugram; the C-D-R-I came in 2019 at the U-N Climate Action Summit in New York, with its secretariat in New Delhi; and the Global Biofuels Alliance and the International Big Cat Alliance followed in 2023.

Prelims Trap. The C-D-R-I's secretariat is in New Delhi; it is the International Solar Alliance that sits in Gurugram.

Pause and choose your code: which of these statements are correct?

The answer is option b, one and three only, because the C-D-R-I was launched in New York in 2019, not at Paris.

Notes card. Four alliances, four years, two cities: copy it.

We are halfway through our journey, so check your score. However many ticks you have, you are learning, and that is what matters. Topic nine: the BRICS Parliamentary Forum, from International Relations.

Why in news: the twelfth BRICS Parliamentary Forum met at Samvidhan Sadan in New Delhi on the twenty-sixth and twenty-seventh of October, chaired by the Lok Sabha Speaker, as India holds the BRICS chair this year.

BRICS now has ten full members: the original five, Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa, plus Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran and the U-A-E, who joined in 2024, and Indonesia, which joined in 2025. Its bank, the New Development Bank, is in Shanghai.

Prelims Trap. Saudi Arabia is not a full member: it was invited, but it has not confirmed, so a statement that lists it is the trap.

Your turn. Count the members in your head first, and then decide: which of these statements are correct?

The answer is option b, one and three only, because Saudi Arabia has not confirmed its membership. Another tick, I hope!

Notes card. Ten members and their joining years: copy them in two columns.

From parliaments to the living planet. Topic ten: the biodiversity summit in Yerevan [Ye-re-VAAN], from Environment.

Why in news: the seventeenth Conference of the Parties to the Convention on Biological Diversity is being held in Yerevan, Armenia, from the nineteenth to the thirtieth of October, with the first global review of the Kunming–Montreal [Kun-ming Mon-tree-awl] framework.

Know the convention's family tree. The convention came from the Rio summit of 1992 and has its secretariat in Montreal; the Cartagena Protocol of 2000 deals with biosafety; the Nagoya Protocol of 2010 with access and benefit-sharing; and the Kunming–Montreal framework of 2022 set twenty-three targets, including thirty by thirty. And here is a link close to home: India hosted the eleventh conference, in Hyderabad, in 2012.

Prelims Trap. Thirty by thirty means protecting thirty per cent of land and thirty per cent of the sea, each, not thirty per cent of the two combined.

Careful with this one; it is a favourite format. How many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is none of them, because thirty by thirty applies to land and sea separately, the venue is Yerevan, and the United States has never ratified the convention. None is a real option in UPSC, so when the facts say none, choose it calmly.

Notes card. The whole family tree on one page: copy it.

And since we are talking about the planet, let us check its temperature. Topic eleven: the Emissions Gap Report, from Environment.

Why in news: U-N-E-P's Emissions Gap Report, released on the twenty-first of October, finds the world heading for about two point eight degrees of warming under current policies, and about two point six degrees even if every pledge is met.

Match each report to its publisher, because this is a classic pairs question. U-N-E-P publishes the Emissions Gap and Adaptation Gap reports; the I-P-C-C publishes the assessment reports; the W-M-O the State of the Global Climate; and the U-N-F-C-C-C secretariat the N-D-C synthesis report.

Prelims Trap. The Emissions Gap Report belongs to U-N-E-P, not to the I-P-C-C. And remember India's own targets: forty-five per cent lower emissions intensity by 2030 against 2005, half its power capacity from non-fossil sources, and net zero by 2070.

Your turn: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is only one: statement two, because U-N-E-P publishes the report, and current policies point to about two point eight degrees.

Notes card. Reports and publishers, plus India's three targets: copy them.

When warming turns into floods, who pays for the damage? Topic twelve: the Loss and Damage Fund, from Environment.

Why in news: on the twenty-second of October, the Board of the Fund for responding to Loss and Damage made its first allocation, a provisional twenty million dollars for Nepal's recovery from the August floods.

Four climate funds sound alike, so let us give each one an address. The Loss and Damage Fund was agreed at COP27 in 2022 and made operational at COP28; the Green Climate Fund came from Cancún in 2010 and sits in Songdo, South Korea; the Global Environment Facility dates from 1991; and the Adaptation Fund comes from the Kyoto Protocol.

Prelims Trap. Loss and damage is paid from its own fund, not from the Green Climate Fund, which pays for mitigation and adaptation.

Pause and try this one: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is only two: statements one and two, because the first allocation went to Nepal, not Bangladesh.

Notes card. Four funds, four addresses: copy them.

From the climate to the wetlands that protect us from its worst. Topic thirteen: India's Ramsar sites, from Environment.

Why in news: on the fifteenth of October, India's Ramsar sites rose to one hundred and three, with Sukhna Wetland in Chandigarh and Sauj Jheel [Saunj Jheel] in Mainpuri, Uttar Pradesh.

Four Ramsar facts to carry with you. The convention was signed at Ramsar in Iran in 1971, and India joined in 1982. India's first sites, in 1981, were Chilika and Keoladeo. The Montreux [Mon-TRUH] Record lists sites under threat, and India's are Keoladeo and Loktak. And the largest Indian site is the Sundarbans.

Prelims Trap. Chilika is no longer on the Montreux Record; it was removed in 2002, so only Keoladeo and Loktak remain.

Your turn, and watch the dates: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is only one: statement two, because Chilika left the record in 2002, and the first sites were Chilika and Keoladeo. Dates and lists like these are where careful readers win marks.

Notes card. Four facts, one wetland page: copy it.

Now a sweet station, quite literally. Topic fourteen: a new G-I tag, as reported, from Art and Culture.

Why in news: Ambaji's Mohanthal [MOHAN-thaal], the temple prasad of Gujarat, has reportedly received a Geographical Indication tag.

Know the G-I law. It comes from the Geographical Indications of Goods Act of 1999, in force from 2003, and the Registry in Chennai works under the Commerce Ministry. A tag lasts ten years and can be renewed again and again, and India's first tag went to Darjeeling tea.

Prelims Trap. A G-I tag is not a permanent, one-time grant: it runs for ten years and must be renewed.

Pause and try this one: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is only two: statements one and two, because the G-I law sits with the Commerce Ministry, not Culture. That is the ministry swap again, and by now you are watching for it.

Notes card. Act, Registry, ten years, Darjeeling: copy it.

From a temple sweet to India's ideas economy. Topic fifteen: the Global Innovation Index, from the Economy.

Why in news: in the Global Innovation Index for 2026, India is ranked thirty-eighth of one hundred and thirty-nine economies, and first among lower-middle-income economies; Switzerland, Sweden and the United States lead.

Pairs on publishers come up every year; remember the pattern from the hunger index? W-I-P-O publishes the Global Innovation Index; U-N-C-T-A-D the World Investment Report; the World Economic Forum the Global Gender Gap Report; and U-N-D-P the Human Development Report.

Prelims Trap. The innovation index is published by W-I-P-O, the World Intellectual Property Organization, not by the World Economic Forum.

Your turn, in the pairs format: how many of these pairs are correctly matched?

The answer is all three, because each report is matched to its real publisher. Publishers, done!

Notes card. Copy the four pairs.

And now our last station, where history and the calendar meet. Topic sixteen: three days to note, from History.

Why in news: three days fall this week: Infantry Day on the twenty-seventh of October, World Savings Day, which India marks on the thirtieth, and Rashtriya Ekta Diwas on the thirty-first.

The static facts behind them. Infantry Day marks the twenty-seventh of October 1947, when troops of the First Sikh landed at Srinagar. Savings Day began in Milan in 1924, and India marks it a day early. And Rashtriya Ekta Diwas, since 2014, marks Sardar Patel's birth anniversary; his Statue of Unity, one hundred and eighty-two metres tall, stands near the Narmada at Ekta Nagar, and that is where our journey ends.

Prelims Trap. India marks World Savings Day on the thirtieth of October, not the thirty-first like most of the world.

Your final question of the day. Make it count: how many of these three statements are correct?

The answer is only one: statement two, because India's Savings Day is the thirtieth, and the statue stands by the Narmada. And that is your last tick of the day.

Your last notes card. Copy it, and your week is complete.
