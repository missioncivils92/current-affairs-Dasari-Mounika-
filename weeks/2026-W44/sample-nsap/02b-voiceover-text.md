## DEEP DIVE 1 — NSAP becomes the PM Samajik Sahayata Yojana

Save as: `sample-nsap_VO_D1.wav`

**💬 TALK TO THE VIEWER — *calm, curious; a smile on "thirty-one years old".***

On the fourteenth of October, the Union Cabinet gave a thirty-one-year-old scheme a new name, the Pradhan Mantri Samajik Sahayata [Saa-maa-jik Sa-haa-ya-taa] Yojana.

It set aside fifty-three thousand four hundred and twenty-eight crore rupees up to 2030–31, and it opened the disability pension to lakhs of people shut out until now.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower on the figures.***

Yet the Centre's old-age pension for a poor seventy-year-old is still two hundred rupees a month, as it was in 2007.

So our central question is this: when a scheme widens who is eligible but not how much they receive, has the safety net really become stronger?

**🎙️ NARRATION**

Fix two terms first.

Social assistance is non-contributory, so the money comes from taxes, not from premiums you paid, unlike the Atal Pension Yojana.

And benchmark disability, from the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, means a certified disability of at least forty per cent.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower.***

Now the static anchor that UPSC marks.

The scheme began on the fifteenth of August, 1995, as the National Social Assistance Programme, a step towards Article 41, which asks the State to give public assistance in old age, sickness and disablement.

Article 41 is a Directive Principle, so no court can enforce it, and the nodal ministry is Rural Development, even though towns are covered too.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

The scheme has five parts, and four get new names: the old-age pension becomes the Vridhavastha [Vri-dhaa-vus-thaa] Pension Yojana, the widow pension the Vidhawa [Vi-dhuh-waa] Pension Yojana, the disability pension the Divyangjan [Div-yaang-jun] Pension Yojana, and the family benefit the Parivar Labh [Pa-ri-vaar Laabh] Yojana.

The fifth, Annapurna [Un-na-poor-naa], ten kilograms of free grain for elderly people without the pension, keeps its name.

Now the real reform.

Until this month the disability pension needed eighty per cent disability and a minimum age of eighteen, so a disabled child got nothing.

The new rule lowers the bar to forty per cent, the 2016 Act's own benchmark, and removes the age limit, so eligibility starts at birth.

Reports put the effect at about eight lakh beneficiaries rising to about twenty-seven lakh.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower on numbers.***

The amounts moved too, but only for disability.

The pension rises from three hundred to three hundred and seventy-five rupees a month up to seventy-nine, and from five hundred to six hundred and twenty-five at eighty and above.

Each is exactly twenty-five per cent higher, because Section 24 of the 2016 Act requires disability assistance to be at least twenty-five per cent above similar schemes.

**🎙️ NARRATION · `EXT`**

To see why this matters, rewind to 1995, when the programme began with three parts: an old-age pension, a family benefit and a maternity benefit.

In 2000 Annapurna was added, and in 2001 the maternity benefit moved out to the Health Ministry, which is why a question that lists a maternity benefit inside today's scheme is a trap.

In 2007 the old-age pension was renamed and the test changed from destitution to the below-poverty-line list, and in 2009 separate widow and disability pensions followed.

The last general rise came in 2012, when the widow and disability pensions went from two hundred to three hundred rupees and the family benefit from ten thousand to twenty thousand.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower, deliberate.***

**Prelims Trap.** UPSC may ask whether the renaming raised every pension, and the trap is that only disability rates changed, while the old-age pension stays at two hundred and five hundred rupees, the widow pension at three hundred, and the family benefit at a one-time twenty thousand.

The second trap is the ministry: this pension is run by Rural Development, not by the disability department.

The third is the count: five components, four renamed, Annapurna unchanged.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

Try one in the exam's own format, and count how many of these three statements are correct.

The answer is only one, the second statement.

The scheme is run by Rural Development, not Social Justice, and the renaming raised only the disability rates, so the old-age pension did not move.

**Mains Lens.** Fiscally, the outlay is about ten thousand seven hundred crore a year, so it buys continuity, not a bigger pension.

Federally, the Centre's two hundred rupees is a small share of what States actually pay, so the name is national and the cost is mostly State.

Socially, the disability fix meets the 2016 Act and the UN disability convention, and demographically, about one in five Indians will be over sixty by 2050, so a frozen pension is a growing problem.

**🎙️ NARRATION · `EXT`**

**Mains Lens.** On the economic and fiscal side, fifty-three thousand four hundred and twenty-eight crore over five years is roughly ten thousand seven hundred crore a year, only a little above what the Centre has been spending, so the money buys continuity rather than a larger pension.

On governance and federal balance, the Centre's two hundred rupees is a small share of what most States actually pay, so the scheme carries a national name while the States carry most of the cost.

On the social side, the disability fix brings the scheme into line with the 2016 Act and with the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, which India ratified in 2007.

And on the demographic side, the UNFPA India Ageing Report of 2023 projects that about one in five Indians will be over sixty by 2050, so a frozen old-age pension is a growing problem, not a shrinking one.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

So what should change?

The Parliamentary Standing Committee on Rural Development asked in 2025 for a substantial increase in pension amounts, and the C-A-G's audit, Report Number Ten of 2023, found missing surveys of eligible people and no social audits in twenty-five States and Union Territories.

So the new guidelines, due within three months, must fix identification as well as eligibility, and for a country at risk of growing old before it grows rich, the floor must keep its value through indexation.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower.***

**Write this down.** Fifteenth of August 1995, Article 41, the Ministry of Rural Development, forty per cent from birth, and the twenty-five per cent rule of Section 24.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

Here is the question I would set: the scheme widens eligibility but not adequacy; critically examine.

Introduce with Article 41 and this month's decision, build the body on eligibility, adequacy, identification and federal cost-sharing, and conclude with indexation and cleaner beneficiary lists.

**💬 TALK TO THE VIEWER — *warm, unhurried.***

**Pause and answer.** Article 41 and the 2016 Act both protect persons with disabilities, so why could only one of them force the twenty-five per cent higher rate?

Hold that thought until the next chapter.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

Three points to carry away.

The name is new, but the scheme is the 1995 programme under Article 41, run by Rural Development.

The real reform is disability: forty per cent, from birth, and rates twenty-five per cent higher.

And the open question is adequacy, because the old-age rate has not moved since 2007.
