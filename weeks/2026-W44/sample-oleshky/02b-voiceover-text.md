**🎙️ NARRATION — *hushed, curious; let the silence breathe.***

At three o'clock on Tuesday morning, Kyiv time, something unusual happened on one stretch of the Dnipro [DNEE-pro] river ... the guns went quiet. Not across the front, not across Ukraine — only around one small town called Oleshky [Oh-LESH-kee].

**💬 TALK TO THE VIEWER — *warm, direct, a half-smile.***

Now, if you are preparing for this exam, your first instinct might be to note the date and move on. But a mentor would stop you right there and ask a better question — why would two armies at war agree to stop firing around one town, while the war everywhere else goes on?

**🎙️ NARRATION**

That is our central question today, and by the end of this class you will see that the answer runs through a river, a destroyed dam, a canal, a nuclear plant — and New Delhi.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *playful.***

But first, a small test for you. **Pause and answer** — which international convention governs warships passing through the Kerch [KERCH] Strait? Hold on to your answer, because it is a trap, and we will spring it later in this class.

## DEEP DIVE 1 — THE OLESHKY TRUCE

Save as: `sample-oleshky_VO_D1.wav`

**— What was agreed —**

**🎙️ NARRATION**

Here is what was agreed. The silence began at three in the morning Kyiv time, four in Moscow. Its purpose is narrow — to deliver food, medicine and water, and to prepare an evacuation for the roughly seventeen hundred to two thousand people still in the town, most of them elderly. The White House credits the American First Lady, Melania Trump, with brokering it, and reports also point to a role for the Vatican.

On the ground, it is being run by the two countries' human-rights commissioners — Dmytro Lubinets [Loo-BEE-nets] for Ukraine and Yana Lantratova [Lan-TRAH-to-va] for Russia — with non-military teams visiting residents and arranging transport for anyone who wants to leave, towards Ukraine or towards Russia.

**— Key terms first —**

**🎙️ NARRATION — *clear, teacherly.***

Before we go further, let us get three words straight, because the examiner certainly will. A humanitarian pause is a short halt in one place, only to let aid in and people out. A ceasefire is wider and more political. And an armistice or a peace agreement is what actually ends the fighting. Oleshky sits firmly on the first rung of that ladder.

**💬 TALK TO THE VIEWER — *light, smiling.***

Think of it like a green corridor for an ambulance in Hyderabad traffic. For a few minutes, one road clears so one vehicle can pass — but nobody would say the city's traffic problem has been solved.

**— Geography first: the river is the frontline —**

**🎙️ NARRATION**

To understand why this narrow strip of land matters, we have to look at the map before we look at the politics. The Dnipro rises in the Valdai [Val-DAI] Hills of Russia, flows through Belarus and Ukraine, and empties into the Black Sea — at about two thousand two hundred kilometres, it is Europe's fourth-longest river.

Near its mouth, the city of Kherson [Kher-SON] sits on the right, or western, bank — and directly across the water, on the left bank, sits Oleshky. When Russian forces withdrew from the right bank in November 2022, the river itself became the frontline — a natural moat between two armies. Oleshky has been under Russian occupation since the very first day of the full-scale invasion.

**— The timeline river: how one dam changed everything —**

Now, let us travel down the river through time, because every event here is connected. In 2014, after Russia annexed Crimea, Ukraine blocked the North Crimean Canal — a canal that had supplied roughly eighty-five per cent of Crimea's water, drawn from a giant reservoir on the Dnipro.

In the first days of the 2022 invasion, Russian forces blew up that blocking dam near Kalanchak [Ka-lan-CHAK], and water flowed to Crimea again. So you see — this was never just a war over land. It was also a war over water.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower, grave.***

Then came the sixth of June, 2023. The Kakhovka [Ka-KHOV-ka] dam, upstream of Oleshky, was breached — and the reservoir behind it, the largest in Ukraine by volume at about eighteen cubic kilometres, began to drain. Ukraine and most independent assessments blamed the Russian forces who controlled the dam; Moscow blamed Ukraine.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

The water rushed downstream. Oleshky, low on the left bank, was reported to be almost entirely flooded, and with the town under occupation and the river a frontline, help could barely reach the people inside. That is the human history behind this week's truce.

And the dam's loss reached far beyond one town. The canal to Crimea ran dry. And upstream, at Enerhodar [E-ner-HO-dar], the Zaporizhzhia [Za-po-RIZH-zhya] nuclear plant — the largest in Europe, with six reactors — lost the reservoir that had supplied its cooling water. Its reactors are shut down, but they still need cooling, and this year the plant has repeatedly lost all its outside power, surviving on diesel generators.

**— Why truces here are so fragile —**

So let us put the whole picture on one map. The river is the frontline, so any movement near it looks like a military move. The water system — the canal and the old reservoir — is itself a strategic prize. The nuclear plant upstream makes every shell a safety question. The civilians inside are mostly old and cannot simply walk out. And trust is so thin that both sides insisted on non-military teams and outside observers. That is why a truce here has to be small, precise and humanitarian — anything bigger would collide with the war itself.

**— India's dilemma —**

Now let us come to the question that General Studies Paper Two actually cares about. How does New Delhi live with a long war, without giving up its strategic autonomy?

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower on the numbers.***

On one side sits energy security. In September, ship-tracking data showed Russia supplying roughly one third of India's crude imports — about one point seven million barrels every day, far ahead of Iraq and Saudi Arabia.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

On the other side sits principle. In Samarkand [Sa-mar-KAND] in 2022, Prime Minister Modi told President Putin that today's era is not an era of war. Yet in February this year, when the General Assembly voted on a resolution calling for peace in Ukraine, India abstained, along with fifty other countries — preferring, in its own words over the years, dialogue and diplomacy to taking sides.

**💬 TALK TO THE VIEWER — *thoughtful, measured.***

So when a tiny humanitarian truce works, it quietly supports India's position — that talking is possible even in the middle of a war. But it also reminds us that one quiet town does not end a war, and that India's balancing act will be tested for a long time yet.

**— Prelims Trap —**

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower, precise.***

**Prelims Trap.** Remember the question I asked you at the start? Here is the catch. The Montreux [Mon-TRUH] Convention of 1936 governs only the Turkish Straits — the Bosphorus [BOS-fuh-rus], the Sea of Marmara and the Dardanelles [Daar-da-NELZ]. It has nothing to do with the Kerch Strait. Kerch was governed by a bilateral treaty of 2003 between Russia and Ukraine — and both countries denounced that treaty in 2023. So the honest answer to my opening question is: no multilateral convention at all.

**🎙️ NARRATION**

And while you are on this map, collect two more traps. The Sea of Azov, behind the Kerch Strait, is the shallowest sea in the world — on average only about seven metres deep. And when you read "Donbas", read it as Donetsk and Luhansk only. Kherson, where Oleshky sits, is not Donbas — and neither is Zaporizhzhia, whose nuclear plant stands on the Dnipro, not the Don.

Two last pieces of fine print. The Dnipro is the fourth-longest river in Europe, not the third — the Volga, the Danube and the Ural come first. And since this is a humanitarian truce, remember that India is a party to the four Geneva Conventions, but has not ratified their two Additional Protocols of 1977.

**— The examiner's chair —**

And this is not a hypothetical. In 2023, UPSC listed six European countries and simply asked how many of them share a land border with Ukraine. Only two did — Hungary and Romania. That is how this examiner thinks. The news chooses the region; a static fact on the map decides your marks. So learn the whole neighbourhood, not just the town in the headline.

**— Mains Lens —**

**Mains Lens.** Economically, this war touches India's fuel, food and fertiliser bills. In governance and law, it tests the UN Charter and humanitarian law, and shows how hard verification is. In security, it puts a river frontline next to Europe's largest nuclear plant. And socially and environmentally, it leaves elderly civilians trapped and a drained reservoir that scientists are still studying.

And here is a proposition the examiner loves to test — humanitarian pauses are stepping stones to peace. On one hand, they build a little trust, keep channels open and test whether verification can work. On the other hand, without a political track behind them, they can freeze the lines on the map, or simply give both armies time to regroup.

**Write this down.** Twenty-seventh October 2026 for the truce, seventeen hundred to two thousand residents, the sixth of June 2023 for the dam, eighteen cubic kilometres of water lost, a canal that once met about eighty-five per cent of Crimea's needs, and a hundred and seven to twelve, with fifty-one abstentions, at the UN in February this year.

**— Way forward —**

So what is the way forward? Article Thirty-Three of the UN Charter already points the way — negotiation, mediation and conciliation. Nuclear safety at Zaporizhzhia needs the International Atomic Energy Agency's principles respected by both sides. Humanitarian access works best through neutral intermediaries such as the Red Cross. And India can keep doing what its strategic autonomy allows — talking to everyone, and speaking for the Global South on food, fuel and fertiliser.

Here is your practice question for this week — a fifteen-marker for General Studies Paper Two. Start with Oleshky and a clear definition of a humanitarian pause. In the body, weigh its significance against its limits, and then bring in India's stake — energy, food, the Global South and nuclear safety. And conclude that pauses only become peace when they are tied to a real political process.

**— Recap and checkpoint —**

**🎙️ NARRATION — *warm, unhurried.***

So let us recap in three lines. The Dnipro is the frontline, and that is why any truce near it must be tiny and humanitarian. The breach of the Kakhovka dam turned a war over land into a war over water and nuclear safety. And India's strategic autonomy is being tested between energy security and principle. Now pause here, copy this map, and fill in the three blanks before I reveal them ... there you go.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *playful.***

**Pause and answer** — name the two straits governed by the Montreux Convention. If you said the Bosphorus and the Dardanelles, you have just avoided a trap that catches a lot of aspirants.

A ceasefire in one town is a door left ajar. It does not end the war, but it reminds both sides — and all of us — that the door exists. As a future civil servant, that is worth remembering. Most of the hardest problems you will face will not be solved in one stroke. They will be solved by people who keep that door open, one careful step at a time.
