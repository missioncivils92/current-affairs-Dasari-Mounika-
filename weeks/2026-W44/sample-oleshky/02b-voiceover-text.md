At three o'clock on Tuesday morning, Kyiv time, officials on both sides said the guns had gone quiet along one stretch of the Dnipro [DNEE-pro] river. Not across the front, and not across Ukraine, but only around one small town on its left bank, called Oleshky [Oh-LESH-kee].

Why can a ceasefire hold in one town while the war everywhere else goes on, and what does that tell India? That is our central question today, and the answer runs through a river, a destroyed dam, a canal, a nuclear plant, and New Delhi.

## DEEP DIVE 1 — THE OLESHKY TRUCE

Save as: `sample-oleshky_VO_D1.wav`

**— What was agreed —**

**🎙️ NARRATION · `HUB`**

Here is what was agreed. The pause is meant to let food, medicine and water in, and to prepare an evacuation for the seventeen hundred to two thousand people still in the town, most of them elderly. The White House credits the American First Lady with brokering it, and some reports mention a role for the Vatican.

**🎙️ NARRATION · `EXT`**

On the ground, it is being run by the two countries' human-rights commissioners, Dmytro Lubinets [Loo-BEE-nets] for Ukraine and Yana Lantratova [Lan-TRAH-to-va] for Russia, with non-military teams visiting residents and arranging transport for anyone who wants to leave, towards Ukraine or towards Russia.

**— Key terms first —**

**🎙️ NARRATION — *clear, teacherly.* · `HUB`**

Let us get four words straight, because the examiner certainly will. A humanitarian pause is a short halt in one place, only to let aid in and people out. A ceasefire is wider and more political. An armistice stops the fighting without settling the dispute, which is why Korea's 1953 armistice never became a peace treaty. Only a peace agreement ends the conflict, and Oleshky sits on the bottom rung.

**💬 TALK TO THE VIEWER — *light, smiling.* · `EXT`**

Think of it as a green corridor for an ambulance in Hyderabad traffic: one road clears so one vehicle can pass, but nobody would say the city's traffic problem has been solved.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower.* · `EXT`**

And the idea is not new in law. Article Seventeen of the Fourth Geneva Convention of 1949 asks the parties to a conflict to try to conclude local agreements that let the wounded, the sick, the infirm and the aged out of besieged or encircled areas. A small, local humanitarian deal like Oleshky's is very much in the spirit of that article.

**— Geography first: the river is the frontline —**

**🎙️ NARRATION · `HUB`**

To understand why this narrow strip of land matters, we have to look at the map before the politics. The Dnipro rises in the Valdai [Val-DAI] Hills of Russia, flows through Belarus and Ukraine, and empties into the Black Sea after about two thousand two hundred kilometres.

Near its mouth, Kherson [Kher-SON] sits on the right bank, and directly across the water, on the left bank, sits Oleshky. Keep this rule: a river's right and left banks are named as you face downstream. When Russian forces withdrew from the right bank in November 2022, the river itself became the frontline, and Oleshky has stayed occupied since the first days of the invasion.

**— The timeline river: how one dam changed everything —**

Now let us travel down the river through time. In 2014, after Russia annexed Crimea, Ukraine blocked the North Crimean Canal, which had carried about eighty-five per cent of Crimea's water. In 2022, Russian forces blew up the blocking dam near Kalanchak [Ka-lan-CHAK], and the water flowed again. So this was never only a war over land; it was a war over water.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower, grave.* · `HUB`**

Then came the sixth of June, 2023, when the Kakhovka [Ka-KHOV-ka] dam upstream was breached, and Ukraine's largest reservoir by volume, about eighteen cubic kilometres, began to drain. Oleshky, low on the left bank, was reported to be almost entirely flooded. Ukraine and most independent assessments blamed the Russian forces who controlled the dam; Moscow blamed Ukraine.

**🎙️ NARRATION · `EXT`**

The water rushed downstream, and with the river a frontline, help could barely reach the people inside. That is the human history behind this week's truce.

**🎙️ NARRATION · `HUB`**

The dam's loss reached far beyond one town. The canal to Crimea ran dry, and upstream at Enerhodar [E-ner-HO-dar], the Zaporizhzhia [Za-po-RIZH-zhya] nuclear plant, Europe's largest, lost the reservoir that supplied its cooling water. Its six reactors are shut down, but their fuel still gives off decay heat, and this year the plant has repeatedly lost all its outside power.

**— Why truces here are so fragile —**

Let us put the whole picture on one map. The river is the frontline, so any movement near it looks like a military move. The water system is itself a strategic prize. The nuclear plant upstream turns every shell into a safety question. The civilians inside are mostly old and cannot simply walk out. And trust is so thin that both sides insisted on non-military teams. So here is the answer to our central question: a truce can hold here because it is small, precise and humanitarian, and costs neither side any ground.

**🎙️ NARRATION · `EXT`**

And Oleshky has precedents in this very war. In 2022, the United Nations and the Red Cross helped evacuate civilians from the Azovstal [A-zov-STAL] steelworks in Mariupol [Ma-ree-OO-pol]. That March, a humanitarian corridor out of Sumy [SOO-mee] brought Indian students home under Operation Ganga, after the Prime Minister spoke to both Presidents. That July, the UN and Türkiye [TUR-kee-yay] brokered the Black Sea Grain Initiative, which held for a year before Russia withdrew. And the IAEA has repeatedly brokered local ceasefires around the Zaporizhzhia plant so that engineers could repair its power lines. Notice the pattern: each deal was narrow, had a neutral broker, and solved one concrete problem.

**— India's dilemma —**

**🎙️ NARRATION · `HUB`**

Now to the question General Studies Paper Two actually cares about: how does New Delhi live with a long war without giving up its strategic autonomy?

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower on the numbers.* · `HUB`**

On one side sits energy security. Ship-tracking data show that in September, Russia still supplied roughly one third of India's crude imports, about one point seven million barrels a day, its lowest level since April but far ahead of Iraq and Saudi Arabia.

**🎙️ NARRATION · `HUB`**

On the other side sits principle. In Samarkand [Sa-mar-KAND] in 2022, Prime Minister Modi told President Putin that today's era is not an era of war. Yet this February, when the General Assembly voted on a resolution for lasting peace in Ukraine, India abstained with fifty others, preferring dialogue and diplomacy to taking sides.

**💬 TALK TO THE VIEWER — *thoughtful, measured.* · `EXT`**

So when a tiny humanitarian truce works, it quietly supports India's position that talking is possible even in the middle of a war. But one quiet town does not end a war, and India's balancing act will be tested for a long time yet.

**— Prelims Trap —**

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower, precise.* · `HUB`**

**Prelims Trap.** Remember my opening question? The Montreux Convention of 1936 governs only the Turkish Straits: the Bosphorus [BOS-fuh-rus], the Sea of Marmara and the Dardanelles [Daar-da-NELZ]. That is why, in February 2022, it was Türkiye [TUR-kee-yay] that invoked Montreux to close them to the warring parties' warships. It has nothing to do with the Kerch Strait, which was covered by a 2003 treaty between Russia and Ukraine that both denounced in 2023. So the answer is no.

**🎙️ NARRATION · `EXT`**

While you are on this map, collect two more traps. The Sea of Azov [AY-zov], behind the Kerch Strait, is the shallowest sea in the world, only about seven metres deep on average. And when you read Donbas, read it as Donetsk [Da-NYETSK] and Luhansk [Loo-HAANSK] only: Kherson, where Oleshky sits, is not Donbas, and neither is Zaporizhzhia, whose nuclear plant stands on the Dnipro, not the Don.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower.* · `HUB`**

One more piece of fine print, since this is a humanitarian truce. India is a party to all four Geneva Conventions of 1949, but it has not ratified their two Additional Protocols of 1977.

**— The examiner's chair —**

**🎙️ NARRATION · `HUB`**

If I were setting this paper, I would not ask about Oleshky at all. I would list six European countries and ask how many share a land border with Ukraine. So learn the whole neighbourhood: Russia, Belarus, Poland, Slovakia, Hungary, Romania and Moldova. The news chooses the region; a static fact decides your marks.

**🎙️ NARRATION · `EXT`**

Let us try one in the exam's own format. Statement one says the Montreux Convention governs passage through the Kerch Strait. Statement two says the Sea of Azov is joined to the Black Sea by the Kerch Strait. Statement three says Kherson oblast is part of the Donbas. How many are correct? Only one, the second, and notice how the two wrong statements are exactly the traps we just set.

**— Mains Lens —**

**🎙️ NARRATION · `HUB`**

**Mains Lens.** Economically, this war touches India's fuel, food and fertiliser bills. In governance and law, it tests the UN Charter, humanitarian law and verification. In security, it puts a river frontline beside Europe's largest nuclear plant. And socially, it leaves elderly civilians trapped beside a drained reservoir.

**🎙️ NARRATION · `EXT`**

And here is a proposition the examiner likes to test: humanitarian pauses are stepping stones to peace. On one hand, they build a little trust, keep channels open and test whether verification can work. On the other hand, without a political track behind them, they can freeze the lines on the map, or simply give both armies time to regroup.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *slower.* · `HUB`**

**Write this down:** eighteen cubic kilometres lost from the Kakhovka reservoir, eighty-five per cent of Crimea's water once carried by the canal, and one hundred and seven to twelve, with fifty-one abstentions, at the General Assembly.

**— Way forward —**

**🎙️ NARRATION · `HUB`**

So what is the way forward? Article Thirty-Three of the UN Charter already names the tools: negotiation, mediation and conciliation. Nuclear safety needs both sides to respect the IAEA's safety principles for the plant, and aid works best through neutral intermediaries like the Red Cross. And India can keep talking to everyone, and speak for the Global South on food, fuel and fertiliser.

Here is your practice question, a fifteen-marker for Paper Two. Open with Oleshky and a one-line definition of a humanitarian pause. In the body, weigh its significance against its limits, then India's stake. And conclude that pauses become peace only when tied to a political process, as Article Thirty-Three envisages.

**— Recap and checkpoint —**

**🎙️ NARRATION — *warm, unhurried.* · `HUB`**

Let us recap in three lines. The Dnipro is the frontline, so any truce near it must be tiny and humanitarian. The Kakhovka breach turned a war over land into a war over water and nuclear safety. And India's strategic autonomy is tested between energy and principle. Now pause, copy this map, and fill in the three blanks ... there you go.

**🎙️ NARRATION — *playful.* · `HUB`**

**Pause and answer** — how did the breach of one dam put both Crimea's water and a nuclear plant's safety at risk?

**🎙️ NARRATION · `EXT`**

Here is the link. The Kakhovka reservoir fed both: it was the source of the North Crimean Canal, and it supplied the cooling water for the Zaporizhzhia plant, so when it drained, both lost their water at once.
