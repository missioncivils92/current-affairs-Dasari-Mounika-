| E.2 | **S02** `[GIS MAP]` Orange slide: why in news + map. | Why in news: on the twenty-eighth of October, Cyclone Arnab crossed the Andhra Pradesh and Yanam coast in the Godavari delta as a very severe cyclonic storm. |
| E.3 | **S03** `[MIND MAP]` Purple slide: how a cyclone forms. | First, how a cyclone forms. It needs warm sea water of about twenty-seven degrees or more, enough Coriolis force, which is why cyclones do not form right at the Equator, weak wind shear, and a low-pressure area to start from. Warm, moist air rises and condenses, and the heat it releases lowers the pressure at the centre, so more air spirals in, anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere. |
| E.4 | **S04** `[MIND MAP]` Blue slide: the naming process. | Now the naming process. Thirteen countries of the W-M-O and ESCAP panel gave thirteen names each, a list of one hundred and sixty-nine adopted in 2020. The I-M-D's centre in New Delhi gives the next name only when a storm reaches cyclonic-storm strength, and a name is never used again. Arnab was Bangladesh's name. |
| E.5 | **S05** `[DATA CHART]` Purple slide: the IMD ladder. | The I-M-D grades storms by wind speed: a cyclonic storm starts at sixty-two kilometres an hour, and a very severe one, like Arnab, runs from one hundred and eighteen to one hundred and sixty-six. |
