# Learning science and audience behaviour: video length for a weekly current-affairs masterclass

Researched 27 Oct 2026. Several primary PDF hosts (MIT CSAIL, Rochester, ASEE PEER, PMC, Frontiers, MPG) could not be fetched from this environment (DNS failures). For those papers, findings below come from abstracts, publisher records and secondary summaries surfaced by search. Each such item is flagged "[secondary — verify against full text]".

## 1. Attention and engagement over video length (Guo 2014 and after; the attention-span myth; segmenting)

### Takeaway
The "6-minute rule" is an **engagement** finding from MOOC log data (Guo et al. 2014), not a learning finding. It does not transfer cleanly to motivated, credit- or exam-driven learners: Lagerstrom et al. (2015) call it a "myth", and Bradbury (2016) finds no primary evidence for a fixed 10–15 minute attention limit. What does hold up well is **segmenting**: a meta-analysis of 56 studies shows a small-to-medium learning gain when a lesson is broken into segments, even when the system sets the pace. Learner-paced chapters inside one long video fit that evidence. The case for "short separate videos" rests mainly on completion data.

### Cited Findings
- Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014), ACM Learning@Scale, pp. 41–50 (DOI 10.1145/2556325.2566239): 6.9 million viewing sessions across four edX courses. Engagement was measured as watch time and whether learners attempted the post-video problems. "Shorter videos are much more engaging." Informal talking-head and Khan-style tablet videos beat studio lecture recordings. Fast, enthusiastic speakers were more engaging. Pre-recorded classroom lectures "do not make for engaging online videos" even when high quality. — [Bibsonomy record](https://www.bibsonomy.org/bibtex/2ea6e7d9bcdcfedf406d8c9843c5d5687/aho); [MIT CSAIL PDF](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)
- Secondary summaries of Guo 2014:
  - median engagement maxed out at about 6 minutes whatever the video length;
  - videos under 6 minutes had near-100% median engagement;
  - 9–12 minute videos had about 50%;
  - 12–40 minute videos had about 20%.
  - The 2013 edX blog version says learners engaged with "less than a quarter" of videos over 12 minutes.
  - [secondary — verify against full text] — [UVA Teaching](https://teaching.virginia.edu/collections/considerations-for-creating-instructional-videos/412); [UBC Flexible Learning](https://flexible.learning.ubc.ca/?p=2852); [TRU copy of Guo 2013 blog](https://eddl.tru.ca/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/EDDL5101_W5_Guo_2013.pdf)
- Guo's study measured engagement, not learning. The NYU commentary notes that the edX study "did not examine the impact of videos on student learning" and that MOOC contexts differ from for-credit courses. — [NYU SPS Nexus, "The six-minute myth"](https://nexus.sps.nyu.edu/post/the-six-minute-myth)
- Lagerstrom, Johanes & Ponsukcharoen (2015), "The Myth of the Six-Minute Rule", ASEE Annual Conference (DOI 10.18260/p.24895):
  - It used Stanford Center for Professional Development logs (working professionals taking for-credit courses).
  - It argues MOOC learners' motivations "are not necessarily congruent" with enrolled students', and that the analysis "reveals that the 'six minute rule'…" (abstract truncated in what I could retrieve).
  - [ASEE PEER](https://peer.asee.org/the-myth-of-the-six-minute-rule-student-engagement-with-online-videos)
- A Boise State page summarises Lagerstrom as finding that students would watch 12–15 minutes when content was highly relevant to their assignments, and recommends staying under 12 minutes. [UNVERIFIED — page's citation details inconsistent] — [Boise State wiki](https://talk.confluence.boisestate.edu/wiki/spaces/NAV/pages/3003711496/Video+Duration)
- Bradbury (2016), "Attention span during lectures: 8 seconds, 10 minutes, or more?", *Adv Physiol Educ* 40(4): 509–513:
  - The most-cited source for a 10–15 minute decline "barely discusses student attention".
  - Attention studies have methodological flaws, and primary data do not support a 10–15 minute limit.
  - Variation in attention is driven more by the teacher than by the format.
  - The "8-second / goldfish" figure traces to a 2015 Microsoft report and has no measurement behind it.
  - [PubMed 28145268](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145268/); [full text, EPALE](https://epale.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/advan.00109.2016.pdf)
- Brame (2016), "Effective educational videos", *CBE—Life Sci Educ* 15(4): es6:
  - It frames video design around cognitive load, engagement and active learning (signaling, segmenting, weeding, matching modality).
  - It notes students often skip large parts of videos.
  - The often-quoted "keep videos to ~6 minutes" advice in summaries of Brame is Brame citing Guo, not new data.
  - [PMC5132380](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/); [UC Merced summary](https://teach.ucmerced.edu/sites/crte.ucmerced.edu/files/documents/effective_videos_brame_summary.pdf)
- Rey, Beege, Nebel, Wirzberger, Schmitt & Schneider (2019), "A meta-analysis of the segmenting effect", *Educ Psychol Rev* 31(2): 389–419:
  - 56 investigations and 88 comparisons.
  - Segmenting gives significant small-to-medium gains in retention and transfer, lowers overall cognitive load and lengthens learning time.
  - The effect holds for system-paced segments too.
  - Learners with higher prior knowledge gained more on retention.
  - Exact d values not retrieved. [secondary — verify]
  - [MPG record](https://is.tuebingen.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting); [DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-018-9456-4)
- Manasrah et al. (2021, ICIT, DOI 10.1109/ICIT52682.2021.9491115), engineering students:
  - The short-video group performed significantly better.
  - It concludes 6–10 minutes is optimal, and that videos under 3 minutes lacked complete information.
  - [abstract-level only — verify] — [Synapse index](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W3185499935)
- Zhu et al. (2022), *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications*, online-flipped engineering course: short videos raised viewing time by 24.7% and final exam score by 9.0% compared with a long-video group. — [PMC9510322](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9510322/)
- Hsin & Cigas (2013), "Short videos improve student learning in online education", *J Comput Sci Coll* 28(5): 253–259. The citation is confirmed, but I could not retrieve its findings. — [DBLP](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/pid/90/2072.html)
- A study of explicit chapter segmentation found knowledge scores rose with learning time for the chaptered video but not for the unsegmented one. Learners used seeking, speed changes and topic skipping. [catalogue abstract only; year and n unknown] — [CAS library record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edb,123292837)

### Inferences
- The short-video literature is mostly engagement and completion data from low-stakes MOOC learners. Exam aspirants on a weekly "replace daily videos" contract are closer to Lagerstrom's motivated professionals, who are more tolerant of length when the content is directly useful.
- The robust lever is segmenting plus signaling, not total length. A 25–35 min video split into self-contained 5–8 min chapters (Deep Dives) and 1.5–3 min chapters (Focus) matches the segmenting evidence. It also lets the YouTube audience behave like a "short video" audience through chapter navigation.
- Guo's 6-minute ceiling and Manasrah's 6–10 min optimum line up with the CLAUDE.md Deep Dive budget of 5–8 min. Deep Dives longer than about 9–10 min should be split into two chapters.
- Guo found that recorded classroom lectures disengage viewers while enthusiastic, fast-paced informal delivery engages them. That supports the scripted, voice-led, 130–140 wpm format over a lecture-capture style.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found that directly compares one long chaptered YouTube video with the same content as separate short videos, measuring learning (not just watch time).
- Exact Rey 2019 effect sizes and Lagerstrom 2015 numbers are unverified (full text unreachable here).
- No YouTube-published study on chapters and audience retention for educational content. Vendor blogs make claims (e.g., "+12% watch time"), but they are unverifiable and I excluded them.

## 2. Interpolated quizzes and breaks in long sessions (Szpunar 2013; Jing 2016)

### Takeaway
Short tests placed between lecture segments reliably improve learning and note-taking. In about 20-minute lectures they roughly halve mind-wandering. In a 40-minute lecture they still improved learning and the integration of ideas across segments, but did not significantly reduce overall mind-wandering. For a 30–60 min masterclass, a "Pause and answer" checkpoint at the end of each pillar is the evidence-based way to make length tolerable.

### Cited Findings
- Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013), *PNAS* 110(16): 6313–6317. Brief memory tests between segments of an online lecture "reduced mind wandering, increased note-taking and improved learning". They also reduced anxiety about a final cumulative test and lowered subjective cognitive load. — [PMC3631699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/); [Harvard Psychology](https://psychology.fas.harvard.edu/publications/interpolated-memory-tests-reduce-mind-wandering-and-improve-learning-online)
- Author interview on a 21-minute statistics lecture:
  - tested students were about half as likely to mind-wander;
  - they were about 3 times as likely to take notes;
  - untested students reported mind-wandering about 40% of the time when probed.
  - [MedicalResearch.com interview](https://medicalresearch.com/interpolated-memory-tests-reduce-mind-wandering-and-improve-learning-of-online-lectures/)
- Harvard Gazette coverage: students tested between each segment "showed a marked drop in mind-wandering", unlike the group allowed to restudy. — [Harvard Gazette, Apr 2013](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2013/04/online-learning-its-different)
- Jing, Szpunar & Schacter (2016), 40-minute, 8-segment public-health lecture:
  - Interpolated testing beat restudy on overall learning and on integrating information across segments, and increased note-taking.
  - A later review notes "no overall reductions in mind wandering relative to a non-testing condition".
  - [Jing 2016 PDF](https://sites.harvard.edu/schacter-memory/files/2022/08/jing2016-1-1.pdf); [review summary, Pan et al. 2020](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PSSB_2020.pdf)

### Inferences
- Eight segments in 40 minutes (about 5 min per segment) is the best-evidenced long-video structure. A 30–35 min masterclass with 3–5 pillar checkpoints plus a cold-open pre-question gives a test every 5–8 min, which is close to that design.
- In-video checkpoints can't force a response, unlike the lab tests. The 5-second countdown card plus the "answer at the top of the next pillar" open loop is a reasonable approximation. The PDF MCQs extend the testing effect after viewing.
- The Trap Vault rapid-fire near the end works as a cumulative retrieval test, which Szpunar links to lower anxiety and better final recall.

### Gaps
- No study found on in-video, non-mandatory pause prompts on YouTube, or on whether viewers actually pause. Lab tests were compulsory.
- Exact 2016 mind-wandering percentages were not retrieved.

## 3. Playback speed (Murphy 2022; Lang 2020; 2025 meta-analyses; prevalence)

### Takeaway
Most students speed-watch: 85% in a UCLA survey, with 1.25x the most common speed in another. Single-study evidence says 1.5x–2x costs little for young adults. Two 2025 meta-analyses, however, find small-to-moderate learning costs from acceleration overall, especially for transfer and for older learners. Scripting at about 135 wpm leaves headroom for 1.25–1.5x viewing, and slowing on numbers and trap lines matters more because many viewers will be at 1.25x+.

### Cited Findings
- Murphy, Hoover, Agadzhanyan, Kuehn & Castel (2022), "Learning in double time", *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 36(1): 69–82, with 231 UCLA undergraduates:
  - Speeds of 1.5x and 2x showed minimal costs against 1x, both immediately and after one week, and performance declined beyond 2x (2.5x).
  - Watching twice at 2x, one week apart, beat watching once at 1x.
  - Students believed slower was better.
  - The authors caution that the result is for a Roman Empire / real-estate lecture and may differ for harder STEM content.
  - [ScienceDaily, Jan 2022](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/01/220111153637.htm); [author PDF](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2022/03/Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-2021-Murphy-Learning-in-double-time-The-effect-of-lecture-video-speed-on-immediate-and.pdf)
- Murphy et al.'s survey: 85% usually watched pre-recorded lectures faster than normal, and 91% thought normal or 1.5x was better for learning than 2x or 2.5x. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/watching-lecture-twice-double-speed-can-benefit-learning)
- Soil-science survey of 468 students: most habitually turn on captions and raise the speed, with 1.25x the most common. There was no harm up to 1.5x, and small but significant negative effects at 2x versus 1.5x. [via search summary — verify] — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/watching-lecture-twice-double-speed-can-benefit-learning)
- Lang, Mirzaei, Chen & Paepcke (2020), "Is faster better? A study of video playback speed", LAK '20 (not *Computers & Education*):
  - MOOC learners were randomly assigned 1.0x or 1.25x default.
  - The 1.25x group attempted more content, got better grades and earned more certificates.
  - [author PDF](https://angusglchen.github.io/files/LAK2020_David_Is.pdf); [Monash record](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/is-faster-better-a-study-of-video-playback-speed/)
- Murphy, Hoover & Castel (2023), *Memory*: younger adults can watch faster without significant memory deficits, while older adults (61–94) are generally impaired at faster speeds. Faster playback reduced mind-wandering. — [PMC10330257](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10330257); [author PDF](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2023/04/The-effect-of-video-playback-speed-on-learning-and-mind-wandering-in-younger-and-older-adults.pdf)
- Huang, Du & Yang (2025), *Frontiers in Psychology* meta-analysis of 12 studies:
  - Accelerated playback raised cognitive load (g = 0.59).
  - It lowered retention (g = −0.41), transfer (g = −0.50) and satisfaction (g = −0.24).
  - The authors flag possible publication bias, and moderators include on-screen teacher presence, subtitles, country and subject.
  - [secondary — verify] — [Frontiers 10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1427609](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1427609/full)
- Tharumalingam et al. (2025), *Educational Psychology Review*: meta-regression of 110 effect sizes from 24 studies. The title states that increasing playback speed "can impair test performance". Speed-specific values were not retrieved. — [Bohrium record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/increasing-video-lecture-playback-speed-can-impair-test-performance-a-meta-analysis/1129892227363897361-10463)
- Yueh et al. (Nov 2025), *Frontiers in Psychology*, three preregistered experiments: quiz scores did not differ by speed, but faster playback increased perceived distortion and modestly reduced satisfaction. — [Frontiers 10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1664580](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1664580/full)

### Inferences
- Assume a large share of aspirants watch at 1.25–1.5x. A 30-minute video then costs about 20–24 minutes of their time, so the effective time cost is lower than the runtime suggests.
- The meta-analytic costs fall on transfer and cognitive load, which is exactly where dense numbers, Article numbers and traps sit. The existing rule to slow to 110–120 wpm on numbers and traps, with the figure on screen, is a mitigation.
- Murphy's "watch twice fast" result supports using the monthly compilation as a second, spaced viewing.
- English is a second language for many aspirants. None of the speed studies tested L2 listeners, so 2x is riskier for this audience.

### Gaps
- No data on playback-speed behaviour among Indian or UPSC learners specifically.
- No speed study on non-native-English listeners or on dense factual (Prelims-style) material.

## 4. Indian aspirant behaviour: mobile, data, study hours, CA time, compilations, note-making

### Takeaway
YouTube in India is overwhelmingly a phone medium: 87% of YouTube time is on mobile (Comscore, H1 2025). Mobile data use averages about 31 GB per user per month, so a 30-minute video is not a data barrier for most viewers. Topper and coaching guidance broadly puts current affairs at about 1 hour a day (newspaper 45–60 min), plus a weekly 2–3 hour consolidation slot and monthly magazine revision. I found no rigorous survey of UPSC aspirants' video or study habits; most of the evidence is anecdotal or from coaching blogs.

### Cited Findings
- Comscore: in H1 2025, Indians spent 87% of YouTube time on mobile, 11% on CTV and 2% on desktop. CTV viewing hours rose 219% from Feb 2022 to Feb 2025. — [Best Media Info](https://bestmediainfo.com/insights/socials-new-prime-time-is-tv-youtube-ctv-time-up-219-in-india-10445366)
- MTM survey: 87% of Indian viewers use YouTube daily, and Indian adults spend 72+ minutes a day on YouTube. — [Free Press Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/tech/87-indians-prefer-youtube-over-traditional-tv-watch-72-minutes-of-it-daily); [Storyboard18](https://www.storyboard18.com/amp/digital/indian-adults-spend-over-72-minutes-a-day-on-youtube-overtaking-tv-80834.htm)
- Nokia MBiT 2026 (for 2025): average mobile data per user above 31 GB a month (27.5 GB in 2024), with 5G at about 47% of traffic. — [Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/technology/indias-average-monthly-mobile-data-usage-per-user-hits-31gb-in-2025-nokia-3950714)
- Ericsson (Nov 2025): 36 GB a month per active smartphone in India in 2025, the highest globally. — [TelecomTalk](https://telecomtalk.info/robust-demand-for-fwa-in-india-ericsson/1001817)

- Topper Priyank Kishore (AIR-61): the newspaper need not take 2–3 hours, 45–60 minutes is enough, stick to one paper, and use monthly magazines mainly for revision. — [IASExamPortal](https://iasexamportal.com/node/46059)

- Jatin Kishore (AIR 2, CSE 2019), via Rau's IAS: newspapers for understanding, magazines for consolidation. — [Recapio digest of Rau's IAS video](https://recapio.com/digest/ias-preparation-strategy-by-upsc-topper-jatin-kishore-air-2-cse-2019-rau-s-ias-by-rau-s-ias-study-ci)
- Coaching guidance: 6–8 hours of effective study a day, with about 1 hour on current affairs. [coaching blog, not survey-based] — [Oswaal](https://oswaalbooks.com/blogs/crack-the-competitive/upsc-toppers-study-plan-what-they-did-differently-to-succeed)
- Weekly consolidation advice from CareerIndia guides:
  - a fixed day (e.g., Sunday) with 2–3 hours of current-affairs revision, organised by topic (Polity, Economy, Environment) rather than by day;
  - another plan: Days 1–5 revising daily notes, Day 6 MCQs, Day 7 weekly consolidation.
  - [CareerIndia weekly revision](https://www.careerindia.com/news/upsc-current-affairs-weekly-revision-strategy-2026-prelims-mains-guide-056377.html); [CareerIndia systematic revision](https://www.careerindia.com/news/upsc-monthly-current-affairs-revision-method-for-prelims-mains-056169.html)
- Coaching sources converge on a hybrid: limited daily reading, short notes, weekly consolidation and monthly revision. They warn against reading without revising. — [PMFIAS](https://www.pmfias.com/?p=725279); [Legacy IAS](https://www.legacyias.com/?p=168669)
- Anecdotal risk: "many aspirants … spend hours watching [video lectures] without actually studying or revising". Some use YouTube only for revision or for hard topics (Budget, Economic Survey, IR). [blog opinion] — [Ensure IAS](https://www.ensureias.com/?p=4504)
- Google–KPMG "Online Education in India: 2021" (May 2017), 3,600 respondents: test preparation was forecast as the fastest-growing segment (64% CAGR, about USD 515 m by 2021), driven by government-job aspirants. The figures are forecasts. — [KPMG PDF](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmg/in/pdf/2017/05/Online-Education-in-India-2021.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Mobile-first design is mandatory.** Big type, keywords-only text and map labels on the map already fit this. Mobile viewing also means frequent interruptions, which strengthens the case for chapters so viewers can resume at a chapter start.
- **Data cost is no longer a real constraint** at about 31 GB a month. A 30-minute 1080p video is about 1–1.5 GB, roughly 3–5% of a typical monthly allowance; at 720p it is less. Some APPSC/TGPSC aspirants in rural areas may still be data-constrained, so the PDF is the low-bandwidth fallback. [estimate, not sourced]
- **Time budget for current affairs:** about 1 hour a day of newspaper plus a 2–3 hour weekly consolidation slot is the standard guidance. A weekly masterclass that "replaces daily videos" fits inside that weekly slot. At 1.25x, a 30–35 min video takes 24–28 min of viewing, and pausing to copy maps, answering checkpoints and the 6–10-page PDF and MCQs fill the rest. That gives roughly 60–90 min of total engagement, about half the weekly slot, leaving room for the aspirant's own newspaper-note revision.
- Against the 5–7 hours a week that daily current-affairs videos of 45–60 min would cost, a 25–35 min weekly video is a credible "time saved" pitch.
- **Weekly beats monthly for this audience** as a primary product. Guidance treats monthly magazines as revision, and the channel's monthly compilation already fills that second-pass role (and matches Murphy's "watch twice" finding).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or large survey found on UPSC/State PSC aspirants' YouTube use, video length preference, playback speed, commute or audio listening, or note-making from videos. The IJRASET 2023 wellbeing survey mentioned in one blog (41.7% report emotional problems) is unverified and off-topic.
- No data on audio-only or background listening of current-affairs videos by Indian aspirants.
- No current per-GB data price for India.
- Topper hour counts are self-reported in interviews and vary widely (45 min to 4 hours of newspaper).

## 5. Recommendation: length and segment budget for a weekly consolidation that replaces daily videos

### Takeaway
The evidence supports a **25–35 minute** weekly masterclass, with about 30 min as the sweet spot, broken into **chapters of no more than about 8 minutes**, with a retrieval checkpoint every 5–8 minutes. Go beyond 35–40 min only in exceptionally dense weeks, and then with stronger chaptering and a recap-retrieval block. This is consistent with the current CLAUDE.md range (typically 20–35 min, no fixed cap).

### Cited Findings
- Segment length: engagement drops sharply after about 6 min in MOOC logs (Guo 2014), and the optimum is 6–10 min in an engineering-course experiment (Manasrah 2021). — [UBC summary](https://flexible.learning.ubc.ca/?p=2852); [Synapse index](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W3185499935)
- Segmenting improves retention and transfer even when system-paced. — [Rey et al. 2019 record](https://is.tuebingen.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting)
- 40-minute, 8-segment lectures with interpolated tests produce better learning and integration. — [Jing et al. 2016](https://sites.harvard.edu/schacter-memory/files/2022/08/jing2016-1-1.pdf)
- No primary evidence for a fixed 10–15 min attention cliff, and the teacher matters more than the format. — [Bradbury 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145268/)
- Weekly 2–3 hour current-affairs consolidation slot in guidance. — [CareerIndia](https://www.careerindia.com/news/upsc-current-affairs-weekly-revision-strategy-2026-prelims-mains-guide-056377.html)
- Speed-watching is common (85% faster than 1x), so the effective viewing time is below the runtime. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/watching-lecture-twice-double-speed-can-benefit-learning)

### Inferences (proposed budget)
| Element | Budget | Evidence basis |
|---|---|---|
| Total runtime | 25–35 min (default about 30); up to about 40 only if the week truly needs it | Fits the weekly 2–3 h slot alongside the PDF, MCQs and revision; Jing 2016's 40-min ceiling with tests |
| Deep Dive chapter | 5–8 min; split into two chapters if over about 9 min | Guo 6-min engagement ceiling; Manasrah 6–10 min |
| Focus chapter | 1.5–3 min | Segmenting; Glimpse/Focus tiers act as natural micro-segments |
| Checkpoint spacing | every 5–8 min (one per pillar plus the cold-open pre-question) | Szpunar 2013; Jing 2016 (about 5-min segments) |
| Chapters (YouTube) | one per pillar plus the News Grid and Trap Vault; a chapter start at every topic change | Segmenting; learner-paced control; mobile interruptions |
| Narration pace | about 135 wpm, slowing on numbers and traps | Headroom for 1.25–1.5x viewing; 2025 meta-analyses show costs at higher speeds |
| Second viewing | Monthly compilation acts as a spaced second pass | Murphy 2022 "watch twice" finding; aspirants use monthly magazines for revision |
| Total weekly learner time | about 60–90 min (video at 1.25x plus pauses, PDF and MCQs) | Inference from the time-budget guidance above |

- Track retention per chapter in YouTube Analytics and compare it with these budgets in the Monday metrics review. Any chapter that consistently loses viewers above the channel average is a candidate for trimming or for moving to a Short.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence on the optimal **total** runtime of a weekly current-affairs consolidation for Indian exam aspirants. The 25–35 min recommendation is an inference from lab, MOOC and guidance sources, and should be validated with the channel's own retention data.
