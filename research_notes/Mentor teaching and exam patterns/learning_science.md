# Learning Science of Effective Instructional Video, Applied to a Voice-Only (Faceless) Current-Affairs Masterclass

Research note for: Decode UPSC with Mounika (18–26 min narrated weekly video, voice-only, UPSC/APPSC/TGPSC aspirants, many of them second-language English users).

Method note: web searches only. Several full-text fetches (PubMed Central, publisher PDFs, university sites) failed with DNS errors in this environment. Many numbers therefore come from abstracts, catalogue records or later papers that cite the original. Each such case is flagged. Anything not confirmed by a found source is listed under Gaps rather than stated as fact.

## 1. Mayer's multimedia principles: what each says, strength of support, and which matter most for an 18–26 min narrated video

### Takeaway
The "reduce extraneous processing" principles (coherence, signalling, redundancy, contiguity) and the "manage essential processing" principles (segmenting, pre-training, modality) all have median effects of roughly d = 0.4 to 1.3 in Mayer's own reviews. For a long narrated video, the highest-leverage principles are **temporal contiguity, coherence/weeding, segmenting, signalling and modality**. Treat the size of these effects with caution: the medians come from Mayer's lab-based reviews of short lessons, and they shift between editions.

### Cited Findings
- Mayer (2017), "Using multimedia for e-learning," *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* 33(5): 403–423, reviews 12 principles. Median effect sizes from the abstract, as reproduced in catalogue records: **multimedia d = 1.67** (5 comparisons); **coherence d = 0.70; signalling d = 0.46; redundancy d = 0.87; spatial contiguity d = 0.79; temporal contiguity d = 1.30; segmenting d = 0.70; pre-training d = 0.46; modality d = 0.72**. — [Mayer 2017 record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_1936208032); [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/jcal.12197)
- Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning (2nd ed., 2014), ch. 12, extraneous-processing principles: **coherence was supported in 23 of 23 tests (median d = 0.86)** and **signalling in 24 of 28 tests (median d = 0.41)**. An earlier edition reported coherence at 1.32 (10 of 11 tests) and signalling at 0.60 (3 of 3). So the estimates have shrunk as the evidence base has grown. — [Cambridge Handbook ch.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/C98AB3A6CE760DD63C048936EA0B3B44)
- Handbook chapter on essential processing: **segmenting was supported in 10 of 10 tests (median d = 0.79)**. — [Cambridge Handbook, segmenting/pre-training/modality ch.](https://cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258)
- Social-cue principles: the **voice principle** (a human voice beats a machine voice) is reported at **d = 0.74** across 5 experiments in 3 studies, as Mayer (2017) is cited in a *Computers & Education* article. The **personalization principle** is reported in Mayer's earlier handbook chapter as **10 of 10 tests, median d = 1.3**. — [Computers & Education article via Unpaywall](https://unpaywall.org/10.1016%2Fj.compedu.2019.103605); [Handbook ch.14 summary](https://clarakucher.opened.ca/?p=303)
- Brame (2016), *CBE–Life Sciences Education* 15:es6, turns the cognitive-load principles into four video practices: **signalling, segmenting, weeding (coherence) and matching modality**. — [Brame 2016, PMC5132380](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/); [UC Merced summary](https://teach.ucmerced.edu/sites/crte.ucmerced.edu/files/documents/effective_videos_brame_summary.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Robust:** coherence/weeding, signalling, temporal contiguity, segmenting and modality. Each has many replications in the same direction. Signalling has the smallest effect (about 0.4–0.46) but is the cheapest to implement.
- **Directly applicable to a voice-only video:** *temporal contiguity* (each visual must appear at the moment its words are spoken, not before or after); *spatial contiguity* (labels sit on the map or diagram, not in a separate legend); *modality* (graphics plus spoken narration rather than graphics plus a paragraph of on-screen text); *redundancy* (do not put the full narration on screen as text next to busy visuals).
- **Pre-training** (d = 0.46) maps directly onto a 20–40 s "key terms" primer at the start of a pillar. Examples: "Article 200: Assent / Withhold / Return / Reserve", or "statutory vs constitutional body". This lets the main explanation run without definitional stops.
- **Caution on magnitude:** these are medians from mostly short, lab-based, system-paced lessons with college students, using Mayer-lab transfer tests. Effects in a real 20-minute YouTube video will probably be smaller. Their direction is the reliable part.

### Gaps
- The 2017 paper's medians for personalization, image, embodiment and generative activity could not be retrieved. The full text was unreachable. Do not quote numbers for these from memory.
- No found source tests these principles specifically in 18–26 min videos or with Indian exam-prep audiences.

## 2. Image principle / instructor presence: does a face help? What compensates for no face?

### Takeaway
Showing the instructor's face does **not** reliably improve learning. Meta-analyses find a null effect, or at most a small effect on retention and none on transfer. A face does raise preference, social presence and motivation. **Dynamic drawing** (visuals that build as the narrator speaks), a human voice and a conversational style recover most of what a face offers. A voice-only format is evidence-compatible.

### Cited Findings
- **Alemdağ (2022)**, *Education and Information Technologies* 27(9): 12713–12742, meta-analysis of 20 experiments: instructor presence had **no significant effect on learning or social presence**, but **increased cognitive load and motivation**. Moderator analysis favoured videos that showed **only the instructor's hand**, and lab settings. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-022-11532-4); [METU repository](https://open.metu.edu.tr/handle/11511/101594)
- **Beege et al. (2023)**, *Educational Research Review*, a series of meta-analyses (35 studies, N = 6,339): a **small significant positive effect on retention, no significant effect on transfer**, and higher social presence, affect and motivation. Prior knowledge, domain and instructor size moderated the effects. — [Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek record](https://www.deutsche-digitale-bibliothek.de/item/PBOMPA7MBJFP5UZOZIZ5TTKBWQG7UUP3?lang=en); [summary in PMC9786415](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9786415/)
- **Henderson & Schroeder (2021)** reviewed 12 articles from 2011 to 2021. As described by Alemdağ, they found neither positive evidence for including instructors nor convincing evidence for leaving them out, on learning, cognitive load or social presence. This is a secondary account. — [Alemdağ 2022 (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-022-11532-4)
- **Kizilcec, Papadopoulos & Sritanyaratana (2014)**, CHI, an eye-tracking study. Learners spent about **41% of viewing time looking at the face** and switched between face and slides about every **3.7 s**. Recall showed **no significant difference** with or without the face. Learners **preferred** the face version and rated it more educational. — [bibbase record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/kizilcec-papadopoulos-sritanyaratana-showingfaceinvideoinstructioneffectsoninformationretentionvisualattentionandaffect-2014); [CiNii](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1360581631179047936?lang=en)
- **Kizilcec, Bailenson & Gomez (2015)**, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, two large field studies on the instructor's face. Results were **mixed across learners**. — [Stanford VHIL](https://vhil.stanford.edu/publications/learning/instructors-face-video-instruction-evidence-two-large-scale-field-studies)
- **Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014)**, L@S, analysed 6.9 million edX viewing sessions. Shorter videos were much more engaging. Informal talking-head videos were more engaging than slides alone. **Khan-style tablet drawings were more engaging.** High-quality pre-recorded classroom lectures were not. The measure was *engagement* (watch time and problem attempts), not learning. — [BibSonomy abstract](https://www.bibsonomy.org/bibtex/2ea6e7d9bcdcfedf406d8c9843c5d5687/aho); [UBC Video Learning Hub](https://blogs.ubc.ca/videolearninghub/?p=569)
- **Fiorella & Mayer (2016)**, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 108(4): 528–546, "Effects of observing the instructor draw diagrams on learning from multimedia messages". Five experiments tested watching diagrams being drawn against pre-drawn diagrams. This is the origin of the **dynamic drawing principle**: learners do better when the instructor draws graphics during the lecture rather than pointing at finished ones. — [search summary incl. Mayer et al. 2020 review](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/doi/10.1007/s11423-020-09749-6)
- **Fiorella, Stull, Kuhlmann & Mayer (2019)**, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, "Instructor presence in video lectures: the role of dynamic drawings, eye contact and instructor visibility". **Dynamic drawings beat static drawings even with the instructor not visible (d = .54)** in Experiment 1. — [bib-bvb record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_2310286686); [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt55v7s9t1/qt55v7s9t1_noSplash_87418a79f71257344483b22e7c67c540.pdf?t=prk1a3)
- A 2020 study (N = 196) found the most benefit from **viewing dynamically generated drawings and then explaining aloud** what was learned. — [search summary, same record set](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/doi/10.1007/s11423-020-09749-6)
- **Teacher gaze cue:** Sharma, Jermann & Dillenbourg (2015, EC-TEL) overlaid the teacher's gaze on MOOC video. It made complex visual content easier to follow. A moving pointer or highlight is the faceless analogue. — [EPFL Infoscience](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/208029)
- **Voice principle:** human voice beats machine voice in Mayer's synthesis (d = 0.74). But Craig & Schroeder (2017) found a **modern TTS voice matched or beat a human voice** on learning, with more transfer. In their 2019 follow-up without a visible agent, the human voice still earned **higher persona ratings** (humanness, engagement). — [Computers & Education via Unpaywall](https://unpaywall.org/10.1016%2Fj.compedu.2019.103605); [Springer review 2022](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10639-022-11255-6)

### Inferences
- For a faceless channel, the evidence says the missing face costs little or no **learning**. What it does cost is **social presence, preference and motivation**, the ingredients of retention and watch time. Compensate on those specific channels:
  1. **Human voice, warm and conversational.** Do not move to TTS. The learning parity of TTS does not extend to persona ratings, and persona drives return viewing.
  2. **Dynamic drawing:** flowcharts, timelines and maps should *build* in sync with the narration. The Decode Desk's "words writing themselves in yellow ink" is a direct application. Evidence: d = .54 with no visible instructor.
  3. **A gaze or attention cue:** an animated pointer, underline or highlight that "goes where Mounika is looking".
  4. **Hand-only visuals:** Alemdağ's moderator found hand-only presence favourable. But the channel rule (CLAUDE.md §1) bans showing her hands. Animated "pen nib" or ink-trail effects are the permissible analogue. This is inference, not tested.
- Guo et al.'s "Khan-style drawings are engaging" plus Fiorella & Mayer's dynamic-drawing results is the strongest combined case for the Decode Desk style.

### Gaps
- Wang & Antonenko (2017) was not found in searches. Its findings (reported elsewhere as better recall and satisfaction for difficult topics with the instructor present) could not be verified.
- The "embodiment principle" effect size (human-like gesture by on-screen agents) was not retrieved.
- No study found tests "animated pen without a hand" against "real hand".

## 3. Concept maps / mind maps: provided vs learner-generated, progressive build-up

### Takeaway
Concept maps help, moderately. Studying a provided map beats studying text or lists (g ≈ 0.28–0.4). **Constructing** a map beats studying one (g ≈ 0.82 against about 0.37). **Animated or progressively built** maps show a large but thinly evidenced advantage (g = 0.74, k = 2). A 2017 update gives an overall g = 0.58.

### Cited Findings
- **Nesbit & Adesope (2006)**, *Review of Educational Research* 76: 413–448: 67 effect sizes from 55 studies (N = 5,818). Effects ranged from small to large depending on how maps were used and on the comparison condition. — [SFU page](https://sfu.ca/~jcnesbit/articles/nesbitadesope2006.htm); [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Learning-With-Concept-and-Knowledge-Maps/99900601055701842)
- Sub-effects of the 2006 paper as cited by later reviews (the original full text was not retrieved): **constructing concept maps g = 0.82**; **studying concept maps g = 0.37**; **studying animated concept maps g = 0.74 (k = 2)**; **studying maps rather than outlines or lists g = 0.28 (k = 10)**; studying instructor- or researcher-made maps rather than text, about **d = .4**. — [TUM Clearinghouse summary of Schroeder et al. 2017](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/CHU_KR-19_ENG_Schroeder-2_2017.pdf)
- **Schroeder, Nesbit, Anguiano & Adesope (2017)**, *Educational Psychology Review*, "Studying and constructing concept maps: a meta-analysis": 142 effect sizes (n = 11,814), **overall g = 0.58** (p < .001). — [SFU Summit](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/17789); [Springer PDF mirror](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/10.1007/s10648-017-9403-9)
- Adesope's dissertation (SFU) studied **verbal redundancy and animated concept maps** together. This is directly relevant to narrated, progressively built maps. — [SFU Summit item 9947](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/17789)

### Inferences
- Build every pillar's summary map **progressively**, node by node in sync with the voice. That combines the animated-map advantage with temporal contiguity and dynamic drawing.
- "Copy this into your notebook" plus a pause cue converts a *provided* map into partial *construction*. The stronger construction effect (g = 0.82) makes this worth doing. A better version: show a **skeleton map with blanks** and ask viewers to fill it in before the reveal. This also adds retrieval practice (§5).
- Put the final full map in the PDF notes so the in-video map can stay minimal (coherence).

### Gaps
- No study found compares "copy the provided map" with "construct your own" in video viewers.
- Mind maps as distinct from concept maps (radial, without labelled links) were not separately quantified in the sources found.

## 4. Dual coding, maps, and data visualisation comprehension

### Takeaway
Words plus relevant graphics outperform words alone by a large margin (the multimedia principle, d = 1.67). For geography-heavy content (corridors, basins, EEZs), maps with labels placed on the map (spatial contiguity, d = 0.79) and shown exactly when spoken (temporal contiguity, d = 1.30) are the main design levers. The graphic must be *relevant*: decorative imagery has the opposite effect (§8).

### Cited Findings
- Multimedia principle, median **d = 1.67** (Mayer 2017). Spatial contiguity **d = 0.79**. Temporal contiguity **d = 1.30**. — [Mayer 2017 record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_1936208032)
- Brame (2016) recommends **matching modality**: use the audio channel for explanation and the visual channel for the diagram, rather than duplicating text. — [PMC5132380](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/)

### Inferences
- Paivio's dual-coding theory is the classic theoretical basis for the multimedia principle. The applied evidence quoted here is Mayer's.
- Data visualisation rule for scripts: say the **one comparison** the chart shows ("Andhra's debt is twice the national average") while that bar animates. Do not narrate every number.
- Maps: add a locator inset (where this is within India) before zooming in, which also acts as pre-training for spatial context. Survey of India boundaries remain mandatory (CLAUDE.md §6).

### Gaps
- No primary source was retrieved on Paivio (1971/1986) or on data-visualisation comprehension research (e.g., graph literacy studies). The report writer should not attribute specific numbers to these.
- No retrieved study is specific to geography learning from maps in video.

## 5. Retrieval practice inside videos: pause-and-answer prompts, interpolated tests, spacing

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice is among the most robust findings in learning science (g ≈ 0.50–0.61 against restudy). Interpolated tests inside a 21-minute online lecture **halved mind-wandering, tripled note-taking and improved final test scores** (Szpunar et al. 2013). Pause-and-answer prompts between pillars are strongly justified for an 18–26 min video.

### Cited Findings
- **Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013)**, *PNAS* 110(16): 6313–6317. Interpolating memory tests between segments of an online lecture **reduced mind-wandering, increased note-taking and improved learning**, and also **reduced anxiety** before a cumulative test. — [Harvard Psychology](https://psychology.fas.harvard.edu/publications/interpolated-memory-tests-reduce-mind-wandering-and-improve-learning-online); [PMC3631699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/)
- From the lead author's interview: in a **21-minute** statistics lecture, tested students were **about half as likely to mind-wander** and **about three times as likely to take notes**. Untested students reported mind-wandering about **40%** of the time when probed. These are interview figures and should be confirmed against the paper. — [MedicalResearch.com interview](https://medicalresearch.com/interpolated-memory-tests-reduce-mind-wandering-and-improve-learning-of-online-lectures/)
- **Rowland (2014)**, *Psychological Bulletin*, testing vs restudy: **g = 0.50, 95% CI [0.42, 0.58]** (159 effect sizes, 61 studies). — [J Exp Psych: Applied 2021 citing Rowland](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PS_2021.pdf); [Frontiers 2018](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full)
- **Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017)**, *Review of Educational Research* 87(3): 659–701: practice testing beats other conditions, reported as **g = 0.61** (272 effect sizes, 118 articles). The g value comes from a secondary source and should be verified. A Frontiers paper characterises the two meta-analyses jointly as medium-to-large effects in the 0.50–0.61 range. — [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rethinking-the-Use-of-Tests-A/99900601154801842); [Frontiers 2018](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full)
- **Chan, Meissner & Davis (2018)**, *Psychological Bulletin* 144: 1111–1146, "Retrieval potentiates new learning": testing boosts learning of *subsequent new* material, **g = 0.44**. **Contested:** Boustani & Shanks's (2022) reanalysis found heavy heterogeneity and publication bias, weak evidence for standard designs, and a small but reliable effect for pre-testing designs. Davis, Chan & Wilford (2017) report a "dark side": **frequent switching** between retrieval and encoding can impair new learning. — [UCL Shanks PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10144434/1/Shanks_Heterogeneity%20and%20Publication%20Bias%20in%20Research%20on%20Test-Potentiated%20New%20Learning_VoR.pdf); [UNF "dark side"](https://scholars.unf.edu/es/publications/the-dark-side-of-interpolated-testing-frequent-switching-between-/)

### Inferences
- **Robust:** retrieval practice improves retention of the tested material. **Moderately supported:** interpolated tests keep attention on later segments. **Contested:** how large the "potentiates new learning" boost is, and whether testing too often backfires.
- Design rule: put **one retrieval checkpoint at the end of each pillar** (every 4–6 min). Do not test every minute. Use the format "Pause now. Which body publishes X? Three seconds..." and give the answer after the beat.
- **Pre-question** at the cold open (a pre-testing design, the variant with the most reliable effect in Boustani & Shanks): pose the Prelims trap question *before* teaching it.
- **Spacing:** recycle the previous week's trap items in the cold open or rapid-fire round, and in the monthly compilation. This is spaced retrieval for free.
- YouTube offers no native in-video quiz, so the prompt must be spoken with an on-screen countdown. The pinned-comment MCQ adds a delayed retrieval opportunity.

### Gaps
- No video-specific meta-analysis of "pause-and-answer" prompts on YouTube was found. Whether viewers actually pause is unmeasured.
- Spacing-effect meta-analyses (e.g., Cepeda et al. 2006) were not retrieved here.

## 6. Segmenting and attention: segment length, chapter markers, signposting, recap

### Takeaway
Segmenting is robust (10 of 10 tests, median d ≈ 0.70–0.79). On MOOC platforms engagement drops sharply past about 6 minutes. An 18–26 min video should therefore be built as **4–5 self-contained segments of about 4–6 min**, each with a clear entry signpost, a checkpoint and a recap. Chapters let viewers navigate between them.

### Cited Findings
- Segmenting principle: 10 of 10 tests, **median d = 0.79** (Handbook). The Mayer 2017 abstract gives **d = 0.70**. — [Cambridge Handbook ch.](https://cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258); [Mayer 2017 record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_1936208032)
- Guo et al. (2014), as summarised alongside Brame (2016): median engagement was **nearly 100% for videos under 6 minutes**, falling to **about 20% for 12–40 min videos**. Brame recommends short videos or pause points. — [Brame summary search](https://teach.ucmerced.edu/sites/crte.ucmerced.edu/files/documents/effective_videos_brame_summary.pdf); [Guo et al. abstract](https://www.bibsonomy.org/bibtex/2ea6e7d9bcdcfedf406d8c9843c5d5687/aho)
- Signalling (cues that highlight structure and key points): **d = 0.41–0.46**, supported in 24 of 28 tests. — [Cambridge Handbook ch.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/C98AB3A6CE760DD63C048936EA0B3B44)

### Inferences
- The Guo data come from MOOC learners, and the measure is watch-time, not learning. Exam-motivated aspirants who chose a long-form weekly video may tolerate longer runtimes. The ~6-minute figure is best read as a **segment** length, not a video length.
- Segmenting in the original studies meant *learner-paced* chunks (a "continue" button). YouTube chapters plus an explicit "pause here" provide a partial equivalent.
- The channel's recurring verbal signposts ("Prelims Trap.", "Mains Lens.", "Write this down.") are textbook signalling. Keep them consistent across weeks so they become schema cues.
- Close each pillar with a **15–20 s recap of three items**, and give the next pillar a one-sentence preview ("advance organiser").

### Gaps
- No found study compares 4-min and 6-min segments inside a single long video.
- No found data on YouTube chapter markers and learning (as opposed to navigation).

## 7. Captions: verbatim vs keyword captions; second-language English learners; verbal redundancy

### Takeaway
For learners whose first language is not English, same-language captions have a **large** positive effect on listening comprehension and vocabulary (Montero Perez et al. 2013). Adesope & Nesbit's (2012) verbal-redundancy meta-analysis finds spoken plus written text beats spoken-only **for low-prior-knowledge learners, system-paced material and picture-free material**. Much of that advantage comes from **displaying key terms**, not full text. With heavy visuals the redundancy principle (d = 0.87) warns against full on-screen text. The best compromise is **keyword on-screen text** in the video, with **optional closed captions** that the viewer can turn on.

### Cited Findings
- **Adesope & Nesbit (2012)**, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 104(1): 250–263, 57 studies, mostly postsecondary students. Spoken+written was **no different from written-only**, and **beat spoken-only**. The advantage appeared for **low prior knowledge**, **system-paced** and **picture-free** materials. **Displaying key terms** from the narration accounted for much of the advantage. — [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Verbal-Redundancy-in-Multimedia-Learning-Environments/99900601157101842)
- **Redundancy principle** (Mayer 2017): with *graphics plus narration*, adding duplicate printed text hurts, **d = 0.87**. — [Mayer 2017 record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_1936208032)
- **Montero Perez, Van Den Noortgate & Desmet (2013)**, *System*, a meta-analysis of 18 studies: a **large effect of captions on L2 listening comprehension and vocabulary acquisition**. Test type moderated listening effects; proficiency did not. — [peeref](https://peeref.com/works/56589650); [CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/323206705.pdf)
- **Montero Perez, Peters & Desmet (2014)**, *ReCALL*, "Is less more? Effectiveness and perceived usefulness of keyword and full captioned video for L2 listening comprehension". The results were not retrieved. — [Cambridge ReCALL](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/is-less-more-effectiveness-and-perceived-usefulness-of-keyword-and-full-captioned-video-for-l2-listening-comprehension/9AD29F86C1752148FDBB500921922223)
- A later meta-analysis reports a moderate captioning effect on L2 listening (**g = .61**, 32 studies). Its authors were not visible in the results, so do not attribute this number. — [MDPI Systems 2022 scoping review/meta-analysis](https://mdpi.com/2079-8954/10/5/133)

### Inferences
- The tension is real. The redundancy principle (native speakers, lab, visuals plus narration) says no full text. The L2 captioning literature says captions help comprehension. These can be reconciled:
  - **Burned-in on-screen text:** keywords only, at most 8 words (already the CLAUDE.md §6 rule). This matches Adesope & Nesbit's key-term finding.
  - **Closed captions (CC):** upload an accurate, human-corrected verbatim SRT so L2 viewers and viewers on fast playback can opt in. Auto-captions garble Indian proper nouns (Polavaram, Sarkaria), so correct them.
- Low prior knowledge plus system pacing (a non-pausable video) is exactly the condition in which spoken+written helped. This supports keyword cards for every new term, Article number and statistic.

### Gaps
- Keyword-vs-full caption results (Montero Perez et al. 2014) were not retrieved.
- No study was found on Indian English-medium aspirants specifically. Indian aspirants vary widely in English listening proficiency, and this is not quantified here.

## 8. Personalization / conversational style, seductive details, emotional design

### Takeaway
Conversational, first- and second-person narration helps (personalization, reported median d ≈ 1.3 in an older Mayer handbook chapter; likely smaller now). **Seductive details**, meaning interesting but irrelevant images, anecdotes or clips, reliably **hurt** learning (g = −0.33 overall). **Emotional design** (pleasant colours, anthropomorphic touches in relevant graphics) gives a small positive effect (d ≈ 0.3–0.39).

### Cited Findings
- **Personalization:** supported in 10 of 10 tests, **median d = 1.3**, in Mayer's handbook chapter on social cues. Ginns et al. (2013) meta-analysed personalised materials and found learning benefits; its pooled value was not retrieved. — [Handbook ch.14 summary](https://clarakucher.opened.ca/?p=303); [Dutke et al. 2016 citing Ginns et al. 2013](https://www.unifr.ch/zelf/de/assets/public/files/PersoenlicheProfile/Dutke,%20Grefe,%20Leopold%20(2016)_Learning%20from%20scientific%20texts_Personalizing%20the%20text%20increases%20transfer%20performance%20and%20task%20involvement.pdf)
- **Sundararajan & Adesope (2020)**, "Keep it coherent: a meta-analysis of the seductive details effect," *Educational Psychology Review* 32(3): 707–734 (58 studies, over 7,500 students): **overall g = −0.33**. The effect was stronger, **g = −0.70**, when details were **static**, in **text-and-image** format, or **placed at the end** of material. The moderator figures come from a secondary summary, and a different summary claims no format moderation, so the moderators are **contested**. — [WSU news](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/); [ACU Inspire summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/seductive-details); [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Keep-it-Coherent-A-Meta-Analysis-of/99900601155501842)
- **Brom, Stárková & D'Mello (2018)**, *Educational Research Review* 25: 100–119, emotional design (facial anthropomorphisms and pleasant colours): **retention d = 0.387, comprehension d = 0.317, transfer d = 0.327, intrinsic motivation d = 0.255**, perceived difficulty d = −0.208, positive affect d = 0.113. A later update found **retention g = 0.35, transfer g = 0.27, comprehension g = 0.29** across 28 articles. — [Brom manuscript PDF](https://artemis.ms.mff.cuni.cz/main/papers/emodesign_manuscript_1rev_180928_FINAL_norev.pdf); [Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.966287/full)
- Coherence principle (exclude extraneous material): d = 0.70–0.86, supported in 23 of 23 tests. — [Cambridge Handbook ch.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/C98AB3A6CE760DD63C048936EA0B3B44)

### Inferences
- The `[HYPERFRAME PROMPT]` cinematic images are the channel's biggest seductive-details risk. Each image must **depict the concept being explained** (the actual dam, the actual corridor), not generic mood art. Never use stock "courtroom" or "handshake" b-roll that carries no information. A useful test: "If the image were removed, would the viewer lose any information?" If not, cut it or keep it under 3 s as a transition.
- **Emotional design is compatible with coherence**, provided the warmth lives in the *relevant* graphic (a pleasant palette, a friendly icon style) rather than in added decorations. The brand palette (yellow, teal, charcoal) counts as legitimate emotional design.
- Cold-open "dynamic trigger" anecdotes are acceptable only if they *are* the event being analysed. Tangential human-interest stories are seductive details.
- Personalization: write "you", "let's", "your answer"; use direct address. This is already in CLAUDE.md §5.

### Gaps
- Ginns, Martin & Marsh (2013) pooled effect size was not retrieved.
- No data on whether cinematic AI imagery behaves as a seductive detail in long-form video.

## 9. Narration speech rate (wpm) and comprehension

### Takeaway
No multimedia-learning study found sets an optimal narration rate. Classic time-compression work places the comprehension decline at around **275–282 wpm**, far above normal speech (~150 wpm). The channel's 130–140 wpm target is conservative and safe, and leaves headroom for viewers watching at 1.25–1.5x. Slower rates help low-proficiency readers and listeners.

### Cited Findings
- Comprehension begins to decline at about **275 wpm** (Foulke & Sticht 1969, as cited), with the largest gaps after about **282 wpm**. Normal speech is about **150 wpm**. — [shkspr.mobi summary](https://shkspr.mobi/blog/2022/01/is-it-faster-to-read-or-to-listen/); [ASHA, auditory comprehension and listening rate](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/jshd.2201.23)
- A scoping review of speech speed in audiovisual learning resources (university students) found mixed results. **4 studies showed better learning at speeds up to 2x, 3 showed no difference, 2 showed worse.** The authors suggest **1x to 1.75x** as favourable, and one included study found benefits below 1x. — [CoDAS scoping review](https://www.scielo.br/j/codas/a/bPThRNyMyKLGwJnvzdgh9tx/?lang=en)
- In reading-while-listening, **slow narration helped low-level readers**, while normal rate suited medium and high readers. — [same search result set; IJLTER](https://ijlter.org/index.php/ijlter/article/download/1327/pdf)

### Inferences
- At 135 wpm, 1.5x playback gives about 200 wpm and 2x gives about 270 wpm. Many aspirants watch at 1.5–2x. A deliberate base pace keeps 2x playback just under the decline threshold. Keep the 130–140 wpm rule.
- Slow down further (about 110–120 wpm) for **dense items**: numbers, Article numbers and trap lines. Add the "..." pauses already in the script grammar. This serves L2 listeners (§7).
- Guo et al. are widely reported to find that speaking rate and enthusiasm matter more than polish. That detail could not be verified from full text here, so it should not be quoted.

### Gaps
- No primary multimedia study links narration wpm to transfer test scores.
- Guo et al.'s (2014) speaking-rate figures could not be verified because the full text was unreachable.

## 10. Synthesis: concrete design rules for script and visuals

### Takeaway
For a voice-only 18–26 min video, the highest-value moves are: segment into 4–6 min pillars with a retrieval checkpoint each; sync visuals to the words and build them progressively; weed decorative imagery; keep on-screen text to keywords, with optional corrected captions; and keep a warm human voice in a conversational style.

### Cited Findings
- See the citations in sections 1–9. Every rule below points back to an effect size cited there.

### Inferences
These rules are inferences from the findings above. The evidence strength is shown in brackets.

**Script**
1. Run each pillar as a 4–6 min segment: signpost, pre-training primer of key terms (about 20–30 s), explanation, **retrieval checkpoint**, then a 3-point recap. [segmenting d ≈ 0.7–0.79; pre-training d = 0.46; retrieval g ≈ 0.5–0.6; Szpunar 2013]
2. Open with a **pre-question** (the trap question) before teaching it. [pre-testing: the most reliable sub-effect in the Boustani & Shanks reanalysis]
3. Use one retrieval prompt per pillar, not more. ["dark side" of over-frequent switching; contested]
4. Use conversational "you/let's" narration and keep the human voice; do not replace it with TTS. [personalization; voice persona]
5. Pace at 130–140 wpm overall and about 110–120 wpm on numbers and traps, with pauses. [speech-rate literature, L2]
6. Weed anything that does not serve a Prelims, Mains or Group-1 outcome. [coherence d = 0.70–0.86]
7. Recycle last week's traps in the rapid-fire round. [spacing; retrieval]

**Visuals**
8. Every visual appears **at the moment** its words are spoken. [temporal contiguity d = 1.30]
9. Put labels on the map or diagram, not in a legend. [spatial contiguity d = 0.79]
10. Build diagrams, flowcharts and concept maps **progressively** in sync with the voice (ink-writing). [dynamic drawing d = .54 without a visible instructor; animated maps g = 0.74, k = 2]
11. Use a moving highlight or pointer as a substitute for gaze. [Sharma et al. 2015]
12. Keep on-screen text to keywords only (at most 8 words), with no narration transcript burned in, and upload human-corrected closed captions. [redundancy d = 0.87; Adesope & Nesbit key-term finding; L2 captions]
13. Every cinematic image must carry concept information. Drop decorative b-roll. [seductive details g = −0.33]
14. Use a consistent warm palette and icon style. [emotional design d ≈ 0.3]
15. End each pillar with a **skeleton concept map** ("copy and fill in"), and put the full map in the PDF. [constructing maps g = 0.82; studying maps g = 0.37]
16. Use the same verbal and visual signpost cards every week. [signalling d = 0.41–0.46]

**Robust vs contested (summary)**
- **Robust:** retrieval practice; coherence and seductive details; temporal contiguity; segmenting; signalling (small but consistent); concept-map benefit; L2 captions helping comprehension.
- **Moderate or less certain:** dynamic drawing (a small number of studies, from one lab); personalization (large in early medians, probably smaller now); emotional design (small).
- **Contested or mixed:** instructor face (null to small for learning; positive for affect); voice principle in the era of modern TTS (learning parity, but a persona gap); "retrieval potentiates new learning" magnitude; seductive-detail moderators; whether long-video engagement findings from MOOCs apply to motivated exam aspirants.

### Gaps
- No found study evaluates these rules together in a long-form YouTube current-affairs video, or with Indian competitive-exam aspirants. The weekly channel analytics (retention curves around checkpoints and chapters) would be the only direct evidence.
- Dunlosky et al. (2013), Clark & Mayer's *e-Learning and the Science of Instruction*, and Paivio's dual-coding texts were not retrieved in this session. Their claims are not cited here.
